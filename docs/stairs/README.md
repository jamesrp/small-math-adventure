# Memory robot: Staircases

October 17, 2026. A group of nine puzzles inside [Memory robot](../robot/README.md), built from Week 50 of the Bellingham math circle (staircases and lengths). They are in the puzzle satchel only, not on the Lantern Road. Nothing here has been played by children, and the Week 50 worksheet is unpiloted. The theme's [review card](https://github.com/jamesrp/math-circle-worksheets/blob/main/plans/review/week-50.md) decided the port (Decision, October 10, 2026): on Memory robot's board with the memory hidden, draw a right-and-up path through a shaded corridor with the fewest turns, with the shaded windows as the certificate for "fewest", which the child points at; then reach the finish in an exact number of steps with left steps allowed. The length invariant and the near-diagonal paradox stay on paper (on a grid the first is visible at a glance and the second is continuous), and there is no live length readout. The packet was last changed on October 6, before the card, so this is built from the current packet.

## The mathematics

The board is the streets of an a × b rectangle, with S at the bottom left, (0, 0), and the flag F at the top right, (a, b). A **staircase** walks the streets from S to F one block at a time, only right or up. Some puzzles have a **strip**: only the corners (x, y) with |a·y − b·x| ≤ D, the edge included, may be used, a band D/a blocks above and below the diagonal SF. A **turn** is a corner where the walk changes direction; a **flat piece** is a straight run to the right. A staircase with h flat pieces has at least 2h − 2 turns (a climb between each two flat pieces), and 2h − 1 or 2h if it also starts or ends going up.

- **Every staircase crosses every column once, on a flat piece.** Column c is the block from c to c + 1 across; a staircase makes exactly a steps right, one through each column. A flat piece on street y covers only corners the strip holds on that street, which are a run of corners from the street's first to its last (its **span**). So a flat piece can cross columns c < d together only if some street's span reaches from c to d + 1.
- **Windows prove the fewest turns.** Call a set of columns **windows** when no street's span holds two of them. Each window needs its own flat piece, so k windows force k flat pieces and at least 2k − 2 turns. On every turns puzzle here the most windows k meet a staircase with exactly 2k − 2 turns, so a staircase and k windows together are the proof: the staircase shows 2k − 2 turns are enough, the windows that fewer are impossible (the builder checks k by enumerating window sets, the validator by a separate search over every set of columns). The card's windows are spans that cover the width, each horizontal run inside one, the end ones half as wide; these windows are their dual, points (columns) that no flat piece holds two of. Both prove the same kind of bound. On all five turns puzzles the columns give the same answer as the guide's length argument for Problem 4 ("a horizontal piece spans at most 40 mm, an end piece at most 20 mm": with fewer turns the flat pieces can't cover the width), which the builder's numbers confirm puzzle by puzzle.
- **The claim can only be right on a staircase with the fewest turns.** The app asks for as many windows as the child's own staircase would need, ⌈T/2⌉ + 1 for T turns, since 2(⌈T/2⌉ + 1) − 2 ≥ T. If that many windows exist, T is at most the fewest, so it is the fewest. On a staircase with more turns there are not enough windows, and every set is refused. On every puzzle here, as it happens, the evenly spaced columns (the first, the last and the rest spread between) are a right set; the end windows' being closer together never decides a claim (see Not yet done).
- **Limits.** A set of columns proves only an even number of turns, 2k − 2, so a strip whose fewest is odd (one that forces the staircase to start or end going up) has no window certificate; every puzzle here is even, which the builder asserts. The windows are a lower bound by counting. The most windows equal the fewest street spans that cover every column, by the duality of intervals on a line (covering by intervals against points no interval holds two of); whether a staircase can use that few flat pieces, with climbs between them inside the strip and no extra climb at the start or end, is checked puzzle by puzzle, not proved for every strip.
- **Exact length: a + b + 2L.** With left steps allowed, and never straight back along the street just walked, a path never meets itself (with no steps down, a path could only revisit a corner by reversing). A path that ends at F has made a more steps right than left and b steps up, so its length is a + b + 2L, where L counts its left steps. A length n is possible only when n − a − b is even and not negative, and only up to what the rectangle or strip allows: in the 5 × 4 rectangle of puzzle 8 the longest path snakes along all five streets, 29 steps, so the lengths are 9, 11, …, 29; in puzzle 9's strip nothing is longer than 23.

**Experiments, conjectures, explanations.** The experiments are the child's staircases and paths. The conjectures come from them: make each flat piece as long as the strip allows; the strip holds less at the ends; one step left costs two boxes. The explanations are the certificates: the windows a child taps, drawn in sun yellow with the flat pieces in pine when the claim is right, and the kept path beside a second one of the same length. The reasons are grown-up conversations in each puzzle's notes. The worksheet's written arguments (Problem 4's "explain why fewer turns cannot work", Problem 5's "could a path be shorter than 280 mm", the paradox of P3 and Grades 4–5 P7) stay on paper.

**Grade bands.** Grade-free in the app. The card rates Problem 4 the best task in every band, with K–1 making as few turns as they can and the older bands proving it; here the proof is the windows, which needs no reading beyond the goal but does need seeing which street a column's flat piece could lie on. Puzzle 1 and the exact-length puzzle 6 may suit children who count to 9 with a grown-up reading the goal; puzzles 4, 5, 8 and 9 ask for planning a whole path before drawing. All of this is untested.

## How a puzzle plays

- **The board.** The streets of the rectangle on Memory robot's paper, with the ring at S, the flag at F and the robot at the end of the walk. In a strip, the gray band with its dotted diagonal; streets the walk may use are drawn in ink, the rest stay faint. The walk is an ink trail with a paper edge. Soft pine dots mark the corners one straight piece can reach from the robot.
- **Moving.** Tap a dot to send the robot straight there, or drag from the robot along the streets (a drag follows the finger a block at a time, the longer way first, and stops at the strip's edge). Tap a corner of the trail, or drag back over it, to take the walk back to there. A tap goes to the nearest corner within half a block; a tap on any other corner does nothing. The arrow keys move one block; Tab reaches the dots, and Enter or Space sends the robot. Undo takes back the last move.
- **Fewest turns (1–5).** One goal line: "Reach the flag with the fewest turns." On the flag, **Fewest** turns on (it is off everywhere else). Pressing it shows every column as a control, none marked, and a row of empty slots, one for each window the child's own staircase needs; the dots go while windows are chosen. A tapped column is shaded ochre and dashed, and fills a slot; a second tap clears it (Tab and Enter or Space work on columns too). A press counts once when it ends, however long it is held. The tap that fills the last slot is the claim, judged at once. Right: the puzzle is solved, the windows turn sun yellow and the staircase's flat pieces are drawn in pine. Wrong: the refusal is a move, saved with the board: one line, "A flat piece can cross two of these." (read out to screen readers), the refused columns outlined in rose, the taps cleared, and Fewest off until the staircase changes. Undo keeps the refusal and takes away only its line and outline; the next Undo takes back a piece of the walk, which ends it. The refusal never says whether the staircase has the fewest turns. Pressing Fewest again, or any move or Undo, leaves an unfinished claim. No turn count and no fewest number is visible (the screen-reader status reads the walk's turns).
- **Exact length (6–9).** One goal line: "Two ways to the flag on the last box." Under the board, one empty box per step; each step fills the next box with its arrow. Left steps are allowed, never straight back along the street just walked, and never past the last box. When the walk reaches the flag on the last box, it is kept as a dashed gray path and the robot starts again; a second, different path that does the same solves the puzzle. The same path again gets "That’s the same path." Reaching the flag early is not a solve; the robot can walk on past it. No step count or length is printed; the boxes are the length.
- **How to play** opens with the definitions ("A staircase goes only right and up along the streets. A turn is a corner where it changes direction, and a flat piece is a straight run to the right." or "Each step is one block right, left or up, and a path never turns straight back along the street it just walked."), then the gestures, with one small worked picture on a board that is not a puzzle: a 4 × 2 strip with a four-turn staircase and its three windows, or a 3 × 2 rectangle with a nine-step path. The rules list is under it; the arguments are in the grown-up notes.
- **Hints.** Before any move, the first hint is the authored nudge ("How far along the bottom street can the robot go before it leaves the strip?"); it is never the claim. Then, in turns puzzles, a walk that can no longer finish with the fewest turns is taken back to the longest start that can ("Take the trail back to 2 across, 1 up."), and otherwise the hint names the next straight piece, keeping the current direction as far as it still allows the fewest ("Go right to 2 across.", "Go up to 3 up."). After a refused claim, the hint first changes the walk, taking back its last piece if the staircase has the fewest turns. On the flag of a fewest staircase the reason comes with the claim: "Press Fewest and tap columns 1, 5 and 9, counting from the left: no flat piece can cross two of them." (choosing, where it can, a set that includes columns already tapped). In exact puzzles, a walk with no finish of the right length (other than the kept path) is taken back to the longest start that has one, and otherwise the next piece of a finish is named. The second level marks the dot, the corner to go back to, Fewest, or the windows on the board; the third offers Apply hint. Hints alone finish every puzzle from a fresh start, after a staircase with too many turns, after a refused claim and after Undo past one, after the same path twice, after reaching the flag early and after random play.

## Rules that keep the record honest

- **The certificate is the child's, and it can fail.** The windows are tapped by the child after a complete staircase, so a fresh board solves nothing. Nothing tints, rings or focuses the right columns until a hint is asked for at the second level, and every column looks alike. Random sets of columns pass on the fewest staircase in 1 of 15 pairs in puzzle 1 (6.7%), 1 of 84 sets in puzzle 2 (1.2%), 2 of 220 in puzzle 3 (0.9%), 13 of 495 in puzzle 4 (2.6%) and 40 of 4,368 in puzzle 5 (0.9%), which the validator checks against a seeded sample; columns in page order never pass; on a staircase with more turns nothing passes. The evenly spaced columns do pass (above, and Not yet done).
- **One claim per walk; the refusal is not an oracle.** A wrong claim is a move: the refused set is saved with the board, kept through Undo (Undo takes away its line and outline, so the board still changes), and Fewest stays off until the walk becomes a different word, as a refused Can't in [Fair shares](../shares/README.md) keeps Can't shut until a card moves. So sets can't be tried one after another: each new claim costs taking back a piece and walking it again. "A flat piece can cross two of these." is true of the tapped columns, and the child can check it on the board; it is the same answer whether or not the staircase has the fewest turns (on any staircase most sets are refused, and on one with too many turns all are), and it prints no number.
- The save is the walk (a word of R, U and, in exact puzzles, L steps) and, in turns puzzles, the right windows or the refused set (and whether its line is showing), or, in exact puzzles, the kept path. Whether Fewest is pressed and which columns are tapped so far is view state, outside the save, cleared by any move, Undo or Restart. Everything drawn is derived from the save, and there is no done flag: a solve is a staircase on the flag with windows that prove it, or two different kept-length paths.
- Only legal moves are accepted: a straight run to a corner of the strip or rectangle, a take-back to a corner of the trail, a dragged or keyed walk that stays legal, never a left step in a staircase, never straight back along a street, never past the last box; Fewest only on the flag, once per walk, with exactly as many windows as that staircase needs, in order and different; nothing after a solve.
- Saved boards are checked against all of this: forged windows, too few or too many, away from the flag or with a staircase that has too many turns; a forged refusal (a right set, the wrong size, out of order, away from the flag, beside windows, or a refusal line with no refusal); a finished exact path that is not kept, a kept path short of the last box, an added `done`.
- Every answer is computed by `scripts/build-stairs.mjs` with the mechanic's searches and checked against the table above, and again by `scripts/validate-stairs.mjs` by other methods.

## Not yet done

- **Children's play.** Nothing here has been tried by children, and the worksheet is unpiloted. Three things to watch: whether children press Fewest at the flag of their first staircase and then look at where flat pieces can reach, or guess sets and then take back a piece to guess again, and whether Fewest being off after a refusal is understood; whether "the fewest turns" with no number is understood, or the slots (one per window the child's staircase needs) are read as a budget; and whether in the exact puzzles children plan with the boxes (two boxes per step left) or wander until the boxes fill short of the flag, which shows only as full boxes and no dots.
- **Evenly spaced windows always pass.** On all five turns puzzles the first column, the last and the rest spread evenly between them (rounded) are a right set, so the obvious first guess wins and the card's half-width end windows never decide a claim. No clean strip fixes this: of every strip |a·y − b·x| ≤ D on boards up to 16 × 16 (and up to 20 wide by 14 high) where the fewest is 2k − 2 and not every staircase has the fewest turns, the only ones where the evenly spaced set fails have a corner within 0.16 of a block (measured square to the edge) of the strip's edge, such as 15 × 12 with D = 27 (only columns 1, 4, 8, 12 and 15 pass). Puzzle 3 is the review's 12 × 6 strip, which has a real staircase choice instead.
- **Corners on the edge, 8 against 9.** The card's contrast is not ported. On paper Problem 4 without its edge needs 9 turns, an odd number windows can't prove (they prove only 2k − 2); on these streets, with the corners on the edge left out, every climb gets shorter and the fewest becomes 12, while the most windows (6) prove only 10. Every strip here counts its edge as inside, as Problem 4 does.
- **Fix 2's narrow strip.** At 10 mm a block the ±7.5 mm strip forces a turn on every street (24 turns, almost no choice), and the card's 16 (or 15) needs corners off the grid, the card's pitfall; not ported.
- **On paper.** The length invariant, the paradox (Problem 3 and Grades 4–5 Problem 7), Grades 4–5 Problems 5 and 6, and the bonus sheet's priced diagonals (bonus Problem 1), which the card says fit beside the exact-length set.
- **No playground.** Free walks on the streets are Memory robot's playground; there is no strip editor.
- **Story.** Not on the Lantern Road; no keeper lines.
- **Grade levels.** Grade-free; not in a K–1, 2–3 or 4–5 trail.
