# Squares (a group in Tile gardens)

October 10, 2026. Twelve puzzles and a playground built from Week 32 of the Bellingham math circle (squares inside rectangles) and its bonus, "Square recipes encore". They are a group in **Tile gardens**, below its Easy, Medium and Hard, and are in the puzzle satchel only, not on the Lantern Road. Nothing here has been played by children, and the Week 32 packets are unpiloted. The theme's [review card](https://github.com/jamesrp/math-circle-worksheets/blob/main/plans/review/week-32.md) says keep, fit B. Its Decision shaped this group: square pieces in Tile gardens rather than a fold into Spring-water Jugs; fewest squares of any size, where the child says Done and the app checks; which rectangles fill with only 2s and 3s, with Can't checked; design goals where the child sizes a rectangle and the app runs the biggest-square rule as the check; and the rule's forced cuts as a playground, not a puzzle. Following the card's pitfalls, nothing asks for a typed gcd, nothing asks for a prediction before showing the answer, no find-every set has a counter, and no fewest count is printed as a budget.

## The mathematics

A rectangle w × h on the square grid is cut into whole squares with edges on the grid lines.

- **The biggest-square rule is Euclid's algorithm.** The rule cuts the largest square from one end and repeats on what is left: from w × h with w ≥ h it cuts an h × h square and leaves (w − h) × h. A number that measures both w and h also measures w − h and h, and the reverse, so every cut keeps the common divisors; the sides shrink until the last square fits exactly. So the **last square has side gcd(w, h)**, and it is the largest square whose equal copies fill the rectangle. The first square has the shorter side. (Week 32, every packet's first page and P3–P4.)
- **The recipe is a continued fraction.** Writing down how many equal squares come in a row, largest first, gives the partial quotients of w / h: 8 × 3 cuts 3, 3, then 2, then 1, 1, the recipe 2, 1, 2, and 8/3 = 2 + 1/(1 + 1/2). A recipe fixes a rectangle's shape, not its size (16 × 6 has the same one); the recipe builds the rectangle backwards from its last squares. (Bonus P4.)
- **Five sizes need 13 × 8.** The number of different sizes is the number of division steps. Sides shrink slowest when every step cuts one square, except the last, which cuts two; then each size is the sum of the next two and the sides are consecutive Fibonacci numbers. Within 15 × 15, 13 × 8 (turned or not) is the only rectangle with five sizes; six need 21 × 13 (Lamé, 1844). (Week 32 grades 4–5 P6.)
- **Biggest first is not fewest.** The rule fills 6 × 5 with 6 squares (a 5 and five 1s); 5 fit (two 3s over three 2s). On 7 × 6 the rule uses 7 and 5 fit; on 13 × 11 the rule uses 8 and 6 fit; on 4 × 3 the rule's 4 is fewest. A lower bound has two parts: the areas must be square numbers adding to w · h, and two squares that don't overlap sit side by side or one above the other, so their sides fit across or down. For 4 × 3, 6 × 5 and 7 × 6 this short case check (in each puzzle's grown-up notes) shows that fewer can't fit; for 13 × 11 it is long, and the app's search tries every way. How few squares tile a rectangle in general has no simple formula (Kenyon, 1996). (Bonus P1–P2.)
- **2s and 3s.** A rectangle with both sides at least 2 fills with 2 × 2 and 3 × 3 squares exactly when both sides are even, or both are multiples of 3, or one side is a multiple of 6 (then strips 2 and 3 tall fill it, since any length from 2 up is 2s plus 3s). The app's searches confirm this rule for every rectangle up to 12 × 12; the group does not claim more than that, and uses only the boards below. (Bonus P3.)
- **The certificate for Can't.** A row (or column) of odd length can't be made of 2s alone, so some 3 × 3 square crosses it. One 3 × 3 square crosses three neighbouring lines, so lines three or more apart need different 3s. At most ⌊w/3⌋ · ⌊h/3⌋ of them fit without overlapping: each 3 × 3 square holds exactly one cell whose column and row are both 2 more than a multiple of 3 (counting from 0), and there are that many such cells. More odd lines, three apart, than 3s fit is a certificate that the rectangle can't be filled. It settles 5 × 2 (row 1; no 3 fits), 5 × 5 (rows 1 and 4; one fits) and 7 × 5 (columns 1, 4 and 7; two fit), and 17 rectangles up to 12 × 12 in all. Some rectangles that can't be filled have no certificate of this kind (7 × 7, 7 × 4, 9 × 5 and others), so no puzzle uses them.

Experiments come first: filling, counting, cutting and sizing. The conjectures children can reach are that biggest first is not always fewest, that a last square of 3 needs sides that are multiples of 3, that odd lines need a 3, and that small squares come last, so a recipe or a run of sizes is built backwards. The explanations are grown-up conversations in each puzzle's notes. By worksheet band: K–1 P5 (four equal squares) is puzzle 1; grades 2–3 P6 (last square 3) is puzzle 6; grades 4–5 P6 (most sizes) is puzzle 12; the bonus problems are the rest.

## How a puzzle plays

- **Placing squares** (fewest, 2s and 3s, four). Drag from one corner of a square to the opposite corner, or tap the two corners; tapping one cell twice gives the smallest square. A first tap marks the corner in pine; a second tap that makes no legal square moves the corner there, or drops it when it was the corner itself. Squares are coloured by size, so equal squares look equal, with their side in Georgia numerals; the rectangle's sides are written along its top and left. Tap a square to lift it; Clear lifts them all. In the four puzzle one tap places a 3 × 3 square with its top-left corner there (kept on the board).
- **Fewest** (2, 4, 8, 11). Squares of any size. **Done** is enabled once the rectangle is full. The app checks that no filling uses fewer squares: if one does, "Fewer squares fit." appears and that count stays answered: Done stays off at that count or more, through Lift, Clear and Undo, so Done-then-Undo is not a free yes/no oracle. No count, budget or target is shown.
- **2s and 3s** (3, 5, 7, 10). Two icons show the 2 × 2 and 3 × 3 squares allowed. The puzzle is solved when the rectangle is full, or by a right **Can't**. Can't comes after a try: it is off until at least one square is on the board. Pressing it puts a tab beside each row (right) and below each column (bottom); the child taps the lines that each need their own 3 × 3 square. A line that can't join gets one short refusal: "2s can fill that row." for an even line, "One 3 by 3 square can cross both." for a line within two of a picked one (or a row with a column picked). When the lines picked are too few and no other can join, "Two 3 by 3 squares fit." (or one) appears with that many 3s drawn dashed where they fit. Tapping a picked tab drops it; pressing Can't again closes the picker. As soon as the lines picked are a certificate, the claim is accepted: the lines stay shaded and the 3 × 3 squares that fit are drawn dashed, over the child's own attempt. A wrong claim never becomes a move, so there is nothing for Undo to escape.
- **Four** (1). Four 3 × 3 squares in a tray. When all four make a rectangle, the app runs the biggest-square rule on it: ink cuts with the last square ringed in ochre, and the rectangle goes onto a shelf of two, up to turning. Lifting a square or Undo keeps what is on the shelf. The goal names two rectangles because there are exactly two; the shelf shows the goal, not a find-every count.
- **Design** (6, 9, 12). A 12 × 12 (or 15 × 15) grid with a rectangle from its top-left corner. Tap a cell, or drag the knob at the rectangle's far corner, to size it; arrow keys move the corner too. **Cut** runs the rule on the rectangle at once and draws its cuts, coloured by size, with the last square ringed. Nothing is cut while sizing, so the child commits to a rectangle before seeing its cuts (the card's pitfall, "showing the gcd before the child commits"). Changing the size clears the cuts. Puzzle 6 keeps each rectangle whose last square is 3 and whose first square is new on a shelf of three; puzzle 9 shows the card (2, 1, 2) as groups of plain squares, largest first, with sizes left out; puzzle 12's goal is one sentence.
- **Copy.** Puzzles 3, 5, 7 and 10 show no objective: the size icons and Can't show the task, and How to play gives the rule. The others show one sentence: "Fill it with as few squares as you can." (fewest), "Make two rectangles with different last squares." (four), "Make three rectangles with last square 3 and different first squares." (6), "Make a rectangle whose cuts follow the card." (9) and "Make a rectangle whose cuts have five different sizes." (12).
- **Hints.** Before any move, the first hint is the authored nudge, which never names the answer or the fewest count. Then: fewest hints keep the child's squares when a fewest filling fits beside them ("Put a 3 by 3 square at row 1, column 1."), else lift the newest square that stops one, and say "Press Done." on a fewest filling; 2s and 3s hints do the same towards a filling, and on a board that can't be filled they first place one square (a try) and then give the reason and the claim: "A row of 5 can't be all 2s, so a 3 by 3 square crosses it. Rows 1 and 4 need different ones, and only one fits. Press Can't and tap both." Four hints lift a square that is in the way and place the missing rectangle where it keeps the most squares; design hints name a rectangle that meets the goal, turned the way the child's is ("Make it 9 by 6."), then "Press Cut.". The second level marks the cell, square or button, and the third offers Apply hint. Hints alone finish every puzzle from a fresh start, after a wrong square, after a refused Done, after Undo, after a wrong rectangle and from random play. Hints and searches are memoised per board; a hint worked out from nothing takes at most a few tens of milliseconds (13 × 11 is the slowest).

**Why the fewest lower bound is not tapped.** The Wave 5 rule asks the child to point at a certificate where one can be pointed at. For fewest, the reason no smaller filling exists is a case check over sums of square numbers and how two big squares clash (and for 13 × 11, a search), not a few cells or lines. So Done is checked by the app, and the argument is in the grown-up notes of puzzles 2, 4 and 8. The 2s and 3s Can't has a tappable certificate, the lines, and the child taps it.

## The playground

A 15 × 15 grid starting with the worksheets' 5 × 2 example. Size a rectangle as in the design puzzles; **Cut** takes the biggest square off one end of what is left, one per press, with the uncut part tinted, until the last square is ringed. Changing the size starts again. There is no goal and no Hint, and it never counts as solved. The card calls tapping through the forced cuts a playground, not a puzzle.

## Rules that keep the record honest

- The save is the squares placed (each [column, row, side], oldest first) with the Done answer and the smallest refused count (fewest), or the claim (2s and 3s), or the rectangles kept (four); or the rectangle's size, whether it is cut and the rectangles kept (design); or the size and the number of cuts (playground). Everything shown, including cuts, refusals and what the shelf holds, is derived from it; the Can't picker's lines and refusals are view state outside the save.
- Only legal moves are accepted: squares inside the rectangle, apart, of the allowed sizes (2 and 3, or 3 in the four puzzle, at most four of them); Lift on a square; Clear with squares down; Done only on a full rectangle with fewer squares than any refused count; Can't only with a square on the board and lines that make a certificate; sizes within the grid; Cut once per rectangle (once per square in the playground); and nothing after a solve.
- Saved boards are checked: squares well formed, inside and apart; Done only on a fewest filling; a refused count only above the fewest; a claim only with a square down and a certificate; rectangles kept only those that four 3s make, each once, and a rectangle of all four on the board kept; design rectangles kept only with last square 3 and different first squares, no more than three, and a cut rectangle that qualifies kept; no unknown or missing fields.
- The answers are computed by `scripts/build-squares.mjs` with the mechanic's searches and checked again by `scripts/validate-squares.mjs` by other methods.

## Not yet done

- **Children's play.** Nothing here has been tried by children, and the Week 32 packets are unpiloted. Three things to watch: whether children on the fewest puzzles search for fewer after "Fewer squares fit." or press Done on every full board as a guess; whether the Can't picker (a tab per line, lines three apart, more lines than 3s fit) is understood without an adult, and whether its refusals read as reasons; and whether the design puzzles lead to planning (multiples of 3, building backwards from the last squares) or to sizing at random and pressing Cut, since Cut is unlimited.
- **Placing by corners.** Drawing a square from corner to corner is new in Tile gardens, whose base puzzles place dominoes by two taps. Whether young children find the two-tap corner, and whether the single tap of the four puzzle should be used everywhere with a size chooser, is untested.
- **More sizes than colours.** Sizes 8 and up share one colour (13 × 11's 11, 15 × 15 designs); the numerals tell them apart.
- **Can't boards without lines.** Rectangles such as 7 × 7 and 7 × 4 can't be filled with 2s and 3s but have no line certificate; a different tappable certificate would be needed before they can be puzzles.
- **One playground per family.** The satchel shows one Playground button per family. If another Tile gardens group adds a playground, the two need a choice of which one the button opens.
- **Story and grade levels.** Not on the Lantern Road; no keeper lines. Grade-free; not yet in a K–1, 2–3 or 4–5 trail.
- **Paper only.** The worksheets' written explanations (why the cut keeps the common measures, the Fibonacci argument, P4's "why these two lists match") stay on paper and in grown-up notes.
