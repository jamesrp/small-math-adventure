# Ball triangles

October 17, 2026. Ten puzzles and a playground built from Week 61 of the Bellingham math circle (triangles on a ball). They are in the puzzle satchel only, not on the Lantern Road. Nothing here has been played by children, and the Week 61 packet is unpiloted. The theme's [review card](https://github.com/jamesrp/math-circle-worksheets/blob/main/plans/review/week-61.md) says keep, and its App fit and Decision shaped this family: a globe the child turns, corners that snap to a 15° grid, shortest arcs, true surface corners and the region drawn by the app. The solves are three right corners, pole–equator triangles that hit a named fraction of the ball, and a lune-pair playground that counts coverings, with "a corner sum of 180° or less leaves no area" as the Can't certificate. Corners are never read from the flat picture, and Problem 7's proof stays on paper. The packet has not changed since the card (last source commit October 6, card October 10).

## The mathematics

A corner sits on a dot of the ball: every 15° of latitude and longitude, 266 dots in all (the two poles and 11 rings of 24). A side is the shorter arc of the great circle through two corners, the shortest way between them on the surface. The triangle is the smaller region inside the three sides. Two opposite corners, or three corners on one great circle, make no triangle; the app refuses those moves. A corner's size is the angle between its two sides on the surface: the angle between the planes of the two great circles.

- **Girard's theorem.** A triangle with corners A, B, C covers (A + B + C − 180°)/720° of the ball. So its corners always add up to more than 180°, by an amount that is its area, and a set of three corners adding up to 180° or less fits no triangle on the ball. Nothing else is needed for the Can't claims here. (For the record, on a perfect ball every set with each corner below 180°, a sum above 180°, and A + B < C + 180° and the same for the other two pairs, fits some triangle; but see the next point.)
- **The pole–equator triangle.** A pole N with A and B on its equator g° apart has corners g°, 90°, 90° and covers g/720 of the ball: the meridians through A and B cut out a lune covering g/360, and the equator halves it. Three right corners (g = 90°) cover exactly 1/8, the octant. Any dot can be the pole: what matters is that the other two corners are a quarter circle from it (puzzles 4, 7 and 9).
- **Two right corners.** If a triangle has right corners at B and C, then A is the pole of the great circle through B and C, and the corner at A equals the side BC. So two right corners force the pole–equator shape, and the third corner can be anything from 15° to 165° on this grid. The validator checks every one of the 3,032,624 triangles on the grid: those whose corners are all multiples of 15° are exactly the pole–equator triangles (624 for each third corner g from 15° to 165°, 528 for g = 90°), and no corner of any grid triangle is a whole number of degrees unless it is a multiple of 15°. The smallest grid triangle covers 0.026/720 of the ball. One consequence for the sets: every set that can be made on this grid has two right corners, and no set adding up to 180° or less has two, so "two right corners" would sort every set in puzzles 3, 6 and 10 as well as the sum does. Only the sum is the reason on any ball (Problem 7's records, such as 60°, 60°, 90°, are real triangles off this grid), so the hints, the Can't argument and the grown-up notes all use the sum, and the notes say so.
- **Lune pairs (the playground).** The two great circles through a corner's sides cut the ball into four lunes; the one holding the triangle and the one opposite it form that corner's pair, covering 2 × A/360 = A/180 of the ball. The three great circles cut the ball into eight regions: the triangle, the opposite triangle and six others. The three pairs cover the triangle and its opposite three times each and every other region once (counts 3, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 3), whatever the regions' sizes. Adding up the pairs' areas, (A + B + C)/180 = 1 + 4f, since the triangle and its opposite (each of area f) are covered twice more than the rest; that is Girard's theorem by its classical proof (Problem 7, which stays on paper).
- **Exactness.** Every coordinate of a dot lies in the field Q(√2, √3) (cos 15° = (√6 + √2)/4), so the app decides every corner and area exactly, with no tolerance: a corner is right when (V×P)·(V×Q) = 0, a corner is 15k° when its squared cosine matches, and an area is k/48 of the ball when Van Oosterom and Strackee's half-angle formula gives E = 15k°. The cards show a corner's exact size ("90°") when it is a multiple of 15° and otherwise "≈" with one decimal, or with more where one decimal would print a whole number: "≈90.02°", never "≈90.0°". The validator finds the grid corner closest to a whole number (77.99996°) and checks that it prints with a decimal.

Experiments come first: moving a corner and watching the cards and the region. The conjectures (two right corners leave the third free; the gap at the pole is the area; the corners always add up to more than 180°) come from the cards and the made sets. The explanations are in each puzzle's grown-up notes; the general proof (Problem 7) stays on paper.

## How a puzzle plays

- **The ball.** An orthographic picture of a paper ball, about 360 px across on a desktop and the card's width on a phone, with ink lines every 15°, the equator darker and a dot at every crossing. The triangle is sun with ink sides, dashed where they run behind the ball; each corner has a small arc on the ball, or a pine square when it is right. Corners are lettered discs: dark when they stay put, a pine ring when chosen, faint when behind the ball.
- **Corner cards.** Above the ball, one card per corner: its letter, its true size drawn flat as a small wedge, and its size from the exact test ("90°", or "≈50.8°"). The picture of the ball is never used for sizes.
- **Moving.** Tap a corner or its card to choose it (the first free corner starts chosen), then tap a dot to move it there; or drag a corner from dot to dot (one move, one Undo). A drag that starts anywhere else turns the ball, and the four arrows under it turn it 15° at a time. Keys: arrows move the chosen corner one dot north, south, east or west (over a pole and off it toward the view), hopping over a dot where it would leave no triangle (so B in puzzle 1 crosses A's meridian), and Shift with an arrow turns the ball. A tap goes to what is drawn on top where it lands: a front corner's disc (the one nearer the viewer first), then the faint disc of a corner behind the ball, which is drawn over the front dots (the tap chooses that corner and turns the ball to it), otherwise whichever is nearer, a front corner or a front dot. A move that would leave no triangle is refused, and a drag doesn't preview one.
- **Solves.** Right corners: all three corners exactly 90°. Fractions: the area exactly the named fraction ("Cover 1/12 of the ball."); the board never shows the area, only the corners. Sets: every set made or rightly claimed.
- **Sets and Can't.** The sets sit on a shelf above the cards. A set is made the moment the triangle's corners are exactly those three sizes, in any order, and stays made (pine) with the corners that made it. **Can't** is off on the starting triangle, so it waits for a move (and is off again after Undo back to the start). Press it, then tap a set: every open set becomes a button, all alike, only while Can't is pressed and can be (Undo ends a half-made claim). A set adding up to 180° or less is accepted: struck through, and the argument drawn under the ball as two fans against a straight line, the child's own triangle's corners side by side (lettered) passing the line, its overshoot in sun, and the set's corners (with their sizes) reaching the line and no further. Past a whole turn (corners adding up to more than 360°) the fan winds inward, smaller each time round, so every part and label still shows. A set that can be made gets "There is a way." and **ends the attempt**: the set is marked in ochre on the shelf, Can't gives way to **Again**, nothing on the board moves, Undo is off, and Again (or Restart) starts afresh, with nothing made. So the other set is never tried next in the same attempt.
- **The playground.** The octant to start, every corner free, and three pair buttons. A pair shades its two opposite lunes in sky and draws the three great circles; each region in view shows how many shaded pairs cover it. Moving a corner keeps the pairs and their counts.
- **Copy.** One objective at most: "Make every corner a right angle.", "Cover 1/12 of the ball." and so on, or "Make a triangle with each set of corners."; the playground has none. How to play opens with what a side and a corner's size are, then the gestures, then Can't.
- **Hints.** Before any move the first hint is the authored idea ("Look at the corners at N and at A. Where could B go to make both of them right?"); it is never a claim. Then each hint moves one corner toward the solution needing the fewest corner moves ("Move B 4 steps east.", "Move C off the pole, 2 steps south."). With sets, hints make the sets that can be made first; then, once only sets adding up to 180° or less are left, they give the reason with the claim: "90°, 45° and 45° add up to 180°. Every triangle you make adds up to more. Press Can't, then tap that set." After a wrong Can't the hint is "Press Again, then make that set." The second level marks the corner and its dot (turning the ball once if the dot is behind), the set or Again, and the third offers Apply hint. Hints alone finish every puzzle from a fresh start, after a wrong move and after a wrong Can't. The authored first hints are neutral: they point at watching the cards or adding up the corners, never at the place, the pole's part or the set to claim. The playground has no hints.

## The Can't certificate

- **What the child points at.** The certificate is the set itself: its three sizes are on the shelf, and adding them shows 180° or less. After Can't the child taps that set, and the app checks it by the rule and draws the argument. The argument is drawn rather than tapped because it is a theorem about every triangle (Girard's): the fans show it on the child's own triangle, which always passes the line.
- **It can fail, and a failure is final.** A wrong Can't ends the attempt, so there is no wiggle-and-retry: the validator follows every random claim sequence from the first chance (one move made, nothing made yet), with a wiggle between right claims, and counts 1 right in 3 in puzzles 3 and 10 and 0 in 3 in puzzle 6, whose three sets can all be made. Each of the three sets puzzles has at least two sets that can be made, so the first claim is a choice among three.
- **The last set.** Of the 7 shelf states short of a solve in each sets puzzle, 3 leave one set open, so a claim there has one tap. In puzzles 3 and 10, 1 of those is the state with every set that can be made made and the flat set left; in puzzle 6 the one set left can always be made. The validator reports these shares. The claim there is still a decision, not a free tap: nothing says how many sets can't be made, puzzle 6 shows that the last open set may well be makeable, and a wrong Can't costs the whole attempt, sets made and all, so the child has to judge the set impossible rather than press Can't because nothing else is left.
- **After an attempt.** Can't waits for a triangle other than the starting one, so the argument is drawn round the child's own triangle; "Press Can't" is never a first hint.

## Rules that keep the record honest

- The save is the corners; for sets also the sets made (each with the corners that made it), the sets rightly claimed, the set whose argument is showing, and the set a wrong Can't named (the attempt is over); in the playground, the pairs shaded. Everything shown is derived from it.
- **Undo takes back moves, not claims.** Undo goes back one board with every set made and claimed kept, and with the argument that board showed: undoing a right claim takes its argument away, and undoing a move after it brings it back, so every Undo visibly changes the board (the validator checks every Undo of random walks against the drawn board). A wrong Can't and Again keep no history: the engine asks the mechanic's `noUndo` right after each move, and it says yes exactly for those two, so Undo can't step back over an ended attempt or bring one back.
- Only legal moves are accepted: a free corner to a dot (a pole only at longitude 0) where it leaves a triangle and no two corners share a dot; Can't only in sets puzzles, off the starting triangle, on an open set, and not in an ended attempt; Again only in an ended attempt, where nothing else is accepted; pairs only in the playground; nothing after a solve.
- Saved boards are checked against all of this: dots on the grid, corners that stay put where they started, a triangle; each made set's corners must make it exactly; only sets adding up to 180° or less can be claimed; an attempt ends only on a set that can be made and isn't, never on the starting triangle; an argument only for a claimed set, never on the starting triangle or in an ended attempt; a set the current corners make must be recorded; no extra or earlier fields (such as a forged `done`, or an old save's `refused`).
- The answers are computed by `scripts/build-globe.mjs` with the mechanic's own functions and checked again by `scripts/validate-globe.mjs` by other methods.
- **Fractions aren't a sweep.** In every fraction puzzle no straight run of one corner from the start, dot by dot north, south, east or west, passes an answer (the validator checks every run): in puzzle 2 B starts south of the equator, in puzzle 5 A and B both start off it, in puzzle 8 no corner starts on a pole and B and C must both move, and puzzle 9's A is on neither.

## Not yet done

Things to watch first: whether children still search dot by dot in the fraction puzzles once the shape is set up (no straight run from the start reaches an answer, but after both corners reach the equator a sweep along it does; the board shows corners but no area); whether they find drag-to-turn and the arrows, and read the dark corners as fixed; whether the Can't on a set feels reasoned (adding its three corners) or like a guess by elimination once the other sets are made; and whether ending the attempt on a wrong Can't feels fair in puzzle 3, which is easy, or too costly once sets are made.
