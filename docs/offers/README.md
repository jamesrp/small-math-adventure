# Take it or pass

October 9, 2026. Ten puzzles and a playground built from Week 60 of the Bellingham math circle (optimal stopping). They are in the puzzle satchel only, not on the Lantern Road. No child has played them; the Week 60 worksheets are unpiloted too. The theme's [review card](https://github.com/jamesrp/math-circle-worksheets/blob/main/plans/review/week-60.md) says keep, App fit B, size M, "but change the idea": two offers of three tickets allow only eight first-offer plans, so live scores would let a child try them all. It asks for complete cards in rows by first offer, each row with a Take/Pass switch; the taken offer lit, the unseen tail faded and no totals; one Best claim, with two towers per row shown after as the certificate; bags where wrong rules fail (passing only the smallest, 0, 2, 6; comparing with the next draw's mean, a three-offer 4 from 0, 4, 6); the 0, 3, 6 tie as a find-every; P4's cards where plans A and B differ on the case engine's shelf; and "make a bag" with two tickets pinned. It warns that cases.md's "every order of the offers" is wrong (offers repeat), and to keep random play and predicting an average out of the solves.

## The mathematics

A bag holds three tickets, each as likely to be drawn as any other, and every offer is a fresh draw (the ticket goes back), so offers can repeat. A player takes an offer, scoring it and stopping, or passes it for good; the last offer must be taken. A **card** lists all n offers of a round in order, the ones never seen after a take included, so the 3ⁿ cards are equally likely, and the plan that scores the most on all of them together has the best average.

- **Rows decide alone.** A first-offer switch changes only the cards starting with that offer, so the best plan makes the best choice in each row. Taking a first x scores x on each of its row's 3ⁿ⁻¹ cards; passing scores at most what the remaining n − 1 offers can score on their own cards. With one offer left that is the bag's total; with m left it is, over the tickets y, the larger of y × 3ᵐ⁻¹ and the m − 1 figure. Divided by the number of cards, this is the best-average recurrence V₁ = mean, Vₘ = mean of max(x, Vₘ₋₁): take x with m offers left when x beats Vₘ₋₁, pass when it is below, and either when they tie.
- **0, 4 and 6.** V₁, V₂, V₃ = 10/3, 40/9, 134/27. With two offers the best plan passes 0 and takes 4 and 6: on the row starting with 4, taking scores 12 and passing 10, and it totals 40 on nine cards. With three offers it passes a first 4 (taking scores 36 on its nine cards, passing 40) and then plays the two-offer plan: 134 on 27 cards. **A 4 flips:** taken with two offers left, passed with three. Comparing a 4 with the next draw's mean (10/3) gets three offers wrong; the comparison is with the best plan for what is left.
- **The other bags.** 0, 5, 6: 44 with two offers, 143 with three, both taking 5; with four offers a first 5 is passed (135 against 143 on its 27 cards), for an average of 448/81. 0, 2, 6: only 6 is taken, 34 in all; passing only the smallest ticket scores 32, so that rule fails. 0, 3, 6: a 3 ties with two offers left (9 against 9), so two plans score 36; with three offers a first 3 is passed and a second 3 ties after each passed first offer, so four plans score 126.
- **Memory doesn't help.** With three offers a plan has a switch for each second offer after each passed first, so it may remember the first offer: 2¹² switch settings, 729 different plans once switches that can't matter are ignored. The best second-offer switches are the same after every passed first offer, because what is left comes from the same bag whatever was passed.
- **A and B.** Plan A takes 4 or 6 whenever it appears; plan B takes only 6 first, then 4 or 6. They score differently on five cards, all starting with 4: on 4 0 0 A scores 4 and B 0, and on 4 0 6, 4 6 0, 4 6 4 and 4 6 6 A scores 4 and B 6. B wins 24 to 20 there and 134 to 130 in all, without winning every card.
- **Ties and flips.** With two offers, taking the middle ticket ties with passing it exactly when three times the middle equals the bag's total. Beside 1 and 9, a third ticket from 0 to 20 makes that tie only at 5 (the middle: 15 against 15) and 17 (9 becomes the middle: 27 against 27). In 0, b, 6 with b from 1 to 5, every best plan passes b as a first offer and takes it as a second only at b = 4: 1 and 2 are passed both times, 5 is taken both times, and 3 is passed first but ties second, so some best plans pass a second 3. The bar only rises as more offers remain, so no ticket is taken first and passed second.

Experiments come first: no total shows until a puzzle is solved. The child sets switches from a rule or by comparing a row's lit offers, then claims. A wrong claim says only "Another plan scores more.": pointing at the row to change would turn every claim into an instruction, the oracle the card warns against, so finding the switch is the child's work (or the hints'). After a solve, two towers per row compare taking with passing, the certificate. The recurrence, the averages and why memory doesn't help are in the grown-up notes; no puzzle asks for a fraction, an average or a written proof. `scripts/validate-offers.mjs` checks every claim above with a separate simulation (cards counted in base 3, each plan turned into a decision table and played card by card, and exact fractions for the averages).

## How a puzzle plays

- **The bag and the board.** The bag's three tickets are at the top with the number of offers. Below, a row for each first offer holds every card starting with it; with three offers each row has a line for each second offer. On a card the offer the plan takes is lit gold, the ones passed are plain and the ones never seen are faded and dashed.
- **Switches.** Each row has a Take/Pass switch (Take filled, Pass plain); tapping changes it, and the row's cards relight at once. With three offers each line has its own switch, open only while its row passes; a shut line fades.
- **Best plan** (puzzles 1–3, 6 and 7). Claims that no plan scores more on all the cards together. A wrong claim says "Another plan scores more." and nothing about where; Best plan greys until a switch changes. A right claim solves the puzzle and shows two towers per row, one block per card as tall as its score: "Take" (the first offer on every card) and "Pass" (the most passing can score on them), the larger in bold.
- **Every best plan** (puzzles 4 and 9). **Keep** checks the plan: one that isn't best is refused the same way; a best plan goes on the shelf, drawn as small rows of switches. **That's all** is the claim, as in the other case families: "There's another." if one is missing. The towers follow.
- **Where plans A and B differ** (puzzle 5). Plans A and B are written above the 27 cards, which are buttons and light nothing: the child applies the plans. Tapping a card keeps it if A and B score differently on it; otherwise "A and B score the same on 6 0 0." and the card is outlined until another is tapped. After That's all every card shows two rows, A's take in gold and B's in green, the five cards ringed, with two towers: where they differ, A 20 and B 24.
- **A ticket for the bag** (puzzles 8 and 10). Two tickets are pinned beside a blank. Tapping a ticket in the menu puts it in the bag, in order, and draws the bag's board with every switch on Take; a new ticket starts the switches again. **Keep** checks the ticket with the plan on the switches, which must be a best plan for that bag: otherwise "Another plan scores more."; with a best plan, a ticket that fails is refused with "No tie." or "Not every best plan passes it first and takes it second.". A refused Keep greys until a switch or the ticket changes. So pressing Keep on every ticket doesn't solve the puzzle: each ticket kept needs its board worked. Then That's all. After it the catalog shows every ticket's bag in columns by what the best plan does with the middle ticket: Middle taken, A tie and Middle passed in puzzle 8; Passed first and second, Passed first, a tie second, Passed first, taken second and Taken first and second in puzzle 10; the answers ringed.
- **Keyboard and screen readers.** Every switch, card and ticket is a button, and focus stays on the one used; after a refused Keep it rests on the first switch, or in puzzles 8 and 10 on the ticket in the bag, where Enter does nothing. A screen reader hears each switch ("First offer 0: take", "After a first 0, second offer 4: take"), each card ("0, 4: takes 4"), the plan in words after each change ("Pass a first 0, take a first 4, take a first 6."), every note and the towers ("First 4: taking scores 12, passing at most 10").
- **Hints.** Before any move, the first hint is the authored nudge. Then hints flip one switch toward the nearest best plan, rows before lines ("Pass a first 0.", "After passing a first 0, take a second 4."), then Best plan, Keep or That's all; in puzzle 5 the next card ("Try 4 0 6."); in puzzles 8 and 10 the next ticket, then switches toward a best plan for its bag. The third level offers Apply hint, and hints lead all the way to a solve.

## The playground

Four bags (0 4 6, 0 2 6, 0 3 6 and 0 5 6) and 2 or 3 offers, fixed during a round. A counter shows for each offer left, the one showing included. **Draw** shows the first offer; **Take** scores it and ends the round, and **Pass** gives it up and shows the next; the last offer can't be passed. Each round lands in the pile of its score as its complete card, the offers it never saw drawn too and faded. Up to thirty rounds, then a note to clear; **Clear** empties the piles, and changing the bag or the offers clears them too. There is no Undo, because a passed offer is gone for good (on the worksheets the partner forbids taking it back), no goal and no Hint, and it never counts as solved. A few rounds can favour the worse plan: that is Problem 2's point, and why the puzzles count every card instead.

## Rules that keep the record honest

- Only legal moves are accepted: no switch on a shut line, no Best plan or Keep again after a refusal until a switch (or, in puzzles 8 and 10, the ticket) changes, no Keep of a plan, card or ticket already kept, no card just refused, no ticket outside the menu or the one already in the bag, no That's all with nothing kept or twice in a row, and no moves after a solve. In the playground, no Draw, bag or number of offers during a round, no Pass on the last offer, no more than thirty rounds and no Undo.
- A save is rejected if its plan isn't a string of switches of the right length, a claimed plan isn't best, a recorded refusal doesn't fit its plan (a plan refused that is best, a ticket refused for its plan with a best plan on the switches, or for its goal without one or when it meets the goal) or sits beside a solve, a kept plan, card or ticket is repeated or doesn't count, That's all was accepted with something missing, a refused card actually counts, or the playground's offers and rounds hold a ticket not in its bag, the wrong number of offers or a taken offer out of range.
- Undo takes back the last action, a refused claim or Keep included, except in the playground.

## Not yet done

- **Children's play.** Nothing here has been tried by children. Things to watch: whether children read a card as one whole round, the faded offers included (the worksheets' p. 2 figure, "the later 1 is unseen", has no counterpart here); whether they compare a row's lit offers, or flip switches and claim at random (two offers allow only eight plans, so random claims can still land on the best one; three offers allow 729); whether "Another plan scores more." without a pointer is too little to go on before the hints; and whether the 27-card boards of puzzles 5–7, 9 and 10 are too long to scan on a phone.
- **K–1.** The card's K–1 route (fix 1) compares take and pass towers row by row; puzzle 1's towers draw that comparison after a solve, but no puzzle is set for K–1 and the board needs reading aloud.
- **Four offers.** Problem 8's four-offer half (81 cards) is in puzzle 7's notes, not a puzzle.
- **Proofs on paper.** Problem 9's argument that an extra offer raises the best average stays on paper, as does the argument that memory doesn't help; Problem 5's average-score explanation is carried by puzzle 6's towers.
- **Equal tickets.** The card asks the app to draw on Problem 9's equal case (a bag whose tickets are all the same, where every plan scores alike and an extra offer adds nothing). It is left out: every bag here has three different tickets, because a board has one row per ticket and the switches are named by them. A bag such as 2, 2, 2 would need rows by position instead.
- **Matches.** Problems 1 and 2 (fixed rules over six partner rounds, and opposite winners) become the playground, with no goal.
- **Story.** Take it or pass is not on the Lantern Road and has no keeper lines.
