# Card machines

October 10, 2026. Eleven puzzles and a playground built from Week 17 of the Bellingham math circle (machine memory). They are in the puzzle satchel only, not on the Lantern Road. Nothing here has been played by children, and the Week 17 worksheet is unpiloted. The theme's [review card](https://github.com/jamesrp/math-circle-worksheets/blob/main/plans/review/week-17.md) says keep, and its App fit and Decision shaped this family: the child drags one R arrow and one B arrow from each circle and taps ✓ or ×; Test compares the machine exactly with the rule and shows one shortest row it gets wrong per press; the budget is the minimum; "can't with fewer" is certified with rows the child chooses and one shared ending per pair, which the app puts after both rows (the card's fix 1 done by the screen); and an "impossible" claim (Can't) is made by pointing at the circle where both rows stop, which the app checks.

The family id is `cardmachines`, not `machines`: that id already belongs to Week 3's Shuffle machines ([docs/machines](../machines/README.md)), which share only the name. The child-facing name is Card machines, symbol ◎.

## The mathematics

A **machine** is a few circles, each with one R arrow and one B arrow to a circle (possibly itself) and a fixed answer, ✓ or ×. A marker starts on circle 1. Each card of a row moves the marker along that card's arrow from the circle it is on; the circle where it stops gives the machine's answer for the row, and with no cards circle 1 answers. A **rule** gives ✓ or × for every row of R and B cards. A machine **follows** the rule when it gives the rule's answer on every row, of every length. These are deterministic finite automata over {R, B} with a start state, and the rules a machine can follow are the regular languages.

- **The circle is the whole memory.** If two rows leave the marker on the same circle, they get the same answer after any **shared ending** (the same cards, in the same order, after both): the next card takes both markers along the same arrow, and so on to the end. This needs only that each circle has exactly one arrow per card.
- **Splitting forces circles apart.** So if some shared ending gives one row ✓ and the other ×, the two rows end on different circles in every machine that follows the rule. If k rows can be split two at a time (one ending per pair), every such machine has at least k circles, by pigeonhole. This is the easy half of the Myhill–Nerode theorem (Trevisan, *Notes on the Myhill–Nerode Theorem*, Stanford CS103 Lecture 15, section 1, Lemmas 2 and 4, as the worksheet guide cites).
- **Exact minima.** A working machine with k circles and k rows split two at a time prove that k is the fewest. For the rules here: 2 circles for "the last card is blue" and "the row has a red card"; m for "the reds make groups of m with none left over" (rows with 0, 1, …, m − 1 reds; adding m − i reds makes the first of a pair with i < j reds a multiple of m and leaves the second j − i over); 3 for "the last two cards are red then blue" (no cards and R, both ×, are split by B; R B is split from both by no cards); 3 for "two reds have come next to each other" (no cards and R split by R); 3 for "reds minus blues make groups of three" (puzzle 4's drawn machine); 4 for "reds and blues both make pairs" (no cards, R, B and R B split by the cards that make the first of a pair even–even).
- **No machine at all.** For "as many reds as blues", the rows with 0, 1, 2, … reds split two at a time (adding i blues balances the row with i reds and not one with more), so no fixed number of circles is enough.
- **Can't is exact, and its proof is a circle.** The other half of Myhill–Nerode, which children are not asked for: two rows that no ending splits end on the same circle of the rule's smallest machine, and so of every right machine with the fewest circles (each is the smallest one renamed). So "no shared ending splits these" is true exactly when both rows stop on one circle of such a machine, and a Can't is accepted only with that circle: the child presses Can't and taps where both rows (with the pair's ending) stop, on the drawn machine in puzzle 4 or on their own machine, once it has passed Test, in puzzle 6. Any other circle is refused. For puzzle 4's rule the circle is the remainder of reds minus blues after threes; for puzzle 6's it is how much of R B the row ends with (nothing, R, or R B).
- **Which machines are right.** A machine that follows the rule with the fewest circles is the smallest machine with circles 2 to k renamed (circle 1 stays the start): there are (k − 1)! of them, and the app accepts every one. There are only 2 machines with one circle, 64 with two and 5,832 with three (5,898 with up to three, the card's warning about wiggling arrows), and 1,048,576 with four.
- **Test.** Test compares the machine with the rule on every row, by following both at once over pairs of circles; two machines with at most n and m circles that disagree do so on a row shorter than n × m. It shows the shortest row the machine gets wrong, and among rows of one length the first with R before B. One row per press, each machine is tested once, and Test is ready only for a machine that gets every row it has already shown right, so a change and its reverse can't buy a second look and the list can only grow.

Experiments come first: build, press Test, follow the row it shows on the machine (its arrows glow and an ochre marker shows where it stops) and change an arrow or a mark. The conjectures (this rule needs a circle for "one red waiting"; two × circles can still be different; four circles are the fewest) come from Test's rows and from the pairs. The explanation that fewer circles can't work is the prove puzzles' rows and endings, which the child makes; the app checks only that every pair is split. The written argument and the general m (grades 4–5 Problem 6) stay in the grown-up notes.

## How a puzzle plays

- **The machine.** Circles on a regular polygon (two side by side, three in a triangle, four in a square), each with its number small at the top and its ✓ (pine, on a pale green circle) or × (ink) large below. A short ink arrow points into circle 1. R arrows are red with a red square chip showing R; B arrows are blue with a blue round chip showing B, as on the worksheet's cards. An arrow back to its own circle is a loop on the outside; two arrows between the same circles bend apart. Arrows not placed yet are short dashed stubs with their chip.
- **Building.** Drag a chip onto a circle to point that arrow there (a dashed rubber line follows the finger, and the circle under it gets a pine ring), or tap the chip (it lifts and gets a pine ring; Escape puts it down) and then tap the circle. Tap a circle with no chip lifted to switch it between ✓ and ×. Enter or Space on a chip and then on a circle works from the keyboard.
- **Test** waits until every arrow is placed and the machine gets every row already in the list right; each machine is tested once, so a change to an arrow or a mark is needed before the next press. A right machine solves a build puzzle. Otherwise the shortest row it gets wrong joins a list under the button, newest first, with the rule's answer beside it (ringed in ochre while the current machine disagrees, which is also when Test waits). The newest row, or one tapped in the list, is traced on the machine: its arrows glow ochre and an ochre marker sits where it stops (dashed while an arrow on its way is missing). The list and the count of presses stay through changes, Undo and Restart (Restart keeps them through the `carryOnRestart` hook this family added to the seam).
- **Splitting pairs** (puzzles 4 and 6). The machine is drawn above the pairs in puzzle 4; in puzzle 6 the child builds it first, and the pairs appear under it, locked, once it passes Test. A sun and a sky marker on the machine show where the chosen pair's two rows (with their ending) stop. Each pair is a card with its two rows one above the other, a bracket, and one ending slot after the bracket that belongs to both rows, then the two answers. Tap the slot to choose the pair; the R, B and ⌫ keys (or the letters R and B and Backspace) add and take back cards, up to six. A pair whose answers differ turns green. **Can't** sits beside the keys: press it (it turns pine and the circles become buttons), then tap the circle where both rows stop. The right circle is accepted: the slot shows "can't" with that circle's number and the pair is done. Any other circle, or any circle for a pair that some ending splits, is refused, and "Keep looking." stays until the ending changes. Can't again, another pair or Escape cancels.
- **Fewer circles are not enough** (puzzles 7 to 11). In 8 to 10 the rows appear once the machine passes Test, the machine stays (locked, a little smaller) and the line above the board changes from the rule to "Show 3 circles are not enough for ✓ when …", the form 7 and 11 use, where it is the objective and there is no machine. Each row has a numbered dot (sun, sky, rose, leaf) and its answer; row 1 is chosen first; tap a row to add cards with the keys. Below come all the pairs of rows, each with one ending slot as in the split puzzles; under a machine a pair names its rows by their dots only, since the rows are just above. Every pair split solves. With a machine, its markers show where each row stops, or the chosen pair's two rows with their ending.
- **Copy.** One line sits above the board: the rule in one sentence ("✓ when the last card is blue."), or in 7 and 11 "Show 2 circles are not enough for ✓ when …"; puzzle 4's is "Add the same cards to both rows to get one ✓ and one ×." Puzzles 6 and 8 to 10, which build a machine and then do more, leave the page objective empty and draw the line in the board (`boardLines`): the rule while building, then "Add the same cards to both rows to get one ✓ and one ×." or "Show 3 circles are not enough for ✓ when …", so one line shows at a time. The budget is the number of circles drawn. How to play has the machine rules, Test's rule, the shared-ending rule, what Can't claims and why split rows show fewer circles are not enough.
- **Hints.** Before any move, the first hint is the authored nudge. Then: in a build, the arrow or mark that differs from the nearest right machine (the renaming that needs the fewest changes; on a tie, the one with no diagonals across the square), in words ("Send circle 2’s R arrow to circle 1.", "Make circle 3 say ✓."), then "Press Test."; for pairs, "R and R R: add R after both.", "R R and B: press Can’t and tap circle 3." or "…: take the last card off the ending."; for rows, a row that shares a circle with an earlier one is reshaped ("Add R to row 2.", "Take the last card off row 3.") and then each pair gets its ending ("Rows 1 and 2: add B after both."). The second level marks the chip and circle, key, row, pair, or Can't and then the circle on the board, and the third offers Apply hint. Hints alone finish every puzzle from a fresh start and after wrong arrows, marks, Tests, Can'ts, rows and endings.

## The playground

A machine of one to five circles, starting as one no puzzle asks for (✓ when the number of cards is even: both arrows cross). − and + remove or add a circle (a new circle's arrows loop; an arrow into a removed circle loops back), and **Mix** makes a random machine with the same number of circles. Arrows and marks change as in the puzzles. Below, a row of up to 12 cards built with R, B, ⌫ and ✕ is traced on the machine with the machine's answer. There is no goal, no Test and no Hint, and it never counts as solved. Running rows through a drawn machine is K–1 Problem 1 and grades 2–3 Problem 1.

## Rules that keep the record honest

- The save is the arrows and marks, whether Test has seen this machine, the rows Test has shown and the count of presses; in split puzzles the endings, the Can'ts kept and the Can'ts refused; in prove puzzles the rows and the endings as well; in the playground the machine and the bench row. Everything shown (answers, markers, traces, green pairs, a Can't's circle) is derived from it.
- Only legal moves are accepted: arrows and marks on the puzzle's circles and only before a solve (and, in puzzles 6 and 8 to 10, only until the machine passes); Test only with every arrow placed, not twice on one machine, and only for a machine right on every row already shown; rows and endings of R and B up to six cards, rows and pairs only once the machine has passed; Can't only on an open pair, naming one of the machine's circles, accepted only for the circle where both rows stop and never once its ending already splits; circles and Mix only in the playground.
- Saved boards are checked: every arrow points to one of the circles; a machine marked as tested has every arrow placed, at least one press, and the shortest row it gets wrong among the rows shown; rows shown are distinct, of R and B, up to 24 cards, and no more than the presses; a kept Can't has rows that no ending splits (its ending stays); a refused Can't has an ending that doesn't split its rows; rows, endings and Can'ts stay empty until the machine passes.
- The answers (fewest circles, witness machines, split endings or Can't, and witness rows and endings) are computed by `scripts/build-cardmachines.mjs` with the mechanic's own methods, and checked again by `scripts/validate-cardmachines.mjs` by other methods (below).
