# Pairs that stay

October 10, 2026. Twelve puzzles and a playground built from Week 27 of the Bellingham math circle (stable pairings and deferred acceptance). They are in the puzzle satchel only, not on the Lantern Road. Nothing here has been played by children, and the Week 27 worksheets are unpiloted. The theme's [review card](https://github.com/jamesrp/math-circle-worksheets/blob/main/plans/review/week-27.md) decided to port it, and its Decision shaped this family: two rows of letters with their strips, tap a circle and a square to pair them; the solves are finding the blocker, finding every pairing that can stay (each rejection needs a tapped blocker, and the child declares That's all), repairing one strip or claiming it impossible, and designing strips checked by enumeration. Blocking pairs are flagged only when the child makes a claim, never live; watching deferred acceptance run is not a solve; the find-every puzzles use four-pair markets; there are letters, not people, and no romance.

## The mathematics

Circles A, B, C, D and squares W, X, Y, Z are paired one to one. Each letter has a **strip**: a ranking of every letter on the other side, with no ties. A circle c and a square s that are not together **block** a pairing when c would rather have s than its partner *and* s would rather have c than its partner. A pairing with no blocking pair **can stay**: it is *stable*.

- **One letter wanting to switch is not enough.** Both must say yes. The guide's misconceptions list (p. 16) expects the one-sided error, and every claim here is checked for it: a claim that is not a blocking pair is answered with who says no.
- **Some pairing can always stay** (Gale and Shapley, 1962). Deferred acceptance finds one: the circles ask down their strips, each square holds the best ask so far and lets the others go. Circles only move down and squares only trade up, so it ends within n² − n + 1 asks (7 for three pairs, 13 for four) in a stable pairing; it gives every circle the best partner it has in any stable pairing, and every square the worst. Week 27 Grades 4–5 run it by hand. Here it is the reason the playground's "Can you make strips where no pairing can stay?" has the answer no, and the validator's second way of finding a stable pairing; it is not a puzzle, because watching it run is prediction (the card's pitfall).
- **There can be several, and none need please everyone.** With two pairs, 2 of the 16 profiles have both pairings stable and the other 14 have exactly one. With three pairs, of the 46,656 profiles 34,080 have one stable pairing, 11,484 two, 1,092 three, and none more (puzzle 11; the validator counts them).
- **Mutual first choices are forced.** Two letters that rank each other first are together in every pairing that can stay, since apart they would block. Peeling such pairs off is how puzzles 8 and 9 are solved by thought rather than search.
- **Blocks multiply.** If circles A, B and squares W, X rank each other ahead of everyone else, and C, D, Y, Z do the same, a pairing that crosses the blocks has a circle and a square of one block paired outside it, and they block; so the stable pairings are the products of each block's, here 2 × 2 = 4 (puzzle 10, Grades 2–3 Problem 4's proof).
- **One strip, and the certificate for Can't.** Rewriting one letter's strip changes only whether pairs through that letter block. So a pairing can be repaired by one strip exactly when all its blocking pairs share a letter, and then that letter putting its partner first does it. Two blocking pairs with no letter in common prove that no single strip can (blocking pairs are edges between circles and squares; edges that pairwise share a letter in a two-sided graph all share one). This is the reason a child must tap after Can't (puzzle 4); the validator checks the equivalence on every two-pair case and on 60,000 three-pair cases.
- **At most three last choices with three pairs.** If a circle and a square in different pairs both have their last choice, each would rather have the other, and they block. So the letters with last choices are all circles, all squares, or one pair; with three pairs, at most three. Three is reached when one side has every first choice and is last on its partner's strip (puzzle 12, Grades 4–5 Problem 6).

What children are meant to discover: checking a pairing means asking both letters (puzzles 1, 2, 5); finding all of them means rejecting each other pairing with a reason, and a reason is a blocking pair (8–10); one strip can stop only the blocking pairs through its letter (3, 4, 7); and strips can be designed backwards from the pairings wanted (6, 11, 12). Experiments are the claims and their answers; the conjectures (mutual first choices are forced, blocks multiply, one shared letter repairs) come from finding every pairing and from failed repairs; the explanations are grown-up conversations in each puzzle's notes. Deferred acceptance, its proofs and the bonus pages stay on paper.

## How a puzzle plays

- **The board.** Circles sit in a row in sun, squares below them in sky, with the pairing drawn as ink lines between the rows. Each circle's strip stands above it and each square's below it, first choice nearest the letter, with rank numerals 1, 2, 3, 4 in a narrow gutter. On every strip the letter's partner is shaded, as on the worksheet. Nothing on the board says which pairs block.
- **Pairing.** Tap a circle and then a square (or a square then a circle), or drag one onto the other: they pair up, and their old partners pair with each other, so the board is always a whole pairing. The first tap rings the letter in pine; a second tap on it lets go.
- **Claims.** In check and repair puzzles the pairing is fixed (its lines are grey) and the same taps claim that a circle and a square block it. A claim is drawn as a dashed line; if they do not block, it turns rust, the letter who would keep its partner is named ("X says no.", or "A and Z say no."), and on its strip the partner it keeps is ringed in pine and the letter it was offered fades. A wanted letter is ringed in ochre. **Stays** claims that no pair blocks; **Can't** (repair) claims that no single strip can make it stay.
- **The lock** (find every, last choices, playground). Stays on a pairing that can stay keeps it. Stays on one that can't is answered "Can't stay. Tap a circle and a square that would both rather be together.", the pairing lines go grey, and the pairing can't change until the child taps a blocking pair: that is the tapped blocker each rejection needs. A wrong tap says who says no, with "Tap a circle and a square that would both rather be together." kept below it. While it is held, Undo takes back wrong claims and nothing else: it never takes back the Stays or the move that made the pairing (any further steps Undo passes over are dropped from its history), so Stays and Undo can't ask for free whether a pairing can stay. Restart still clears everything, shelf included.
- **Find every** (puzzles 8 to 10). Each pairing kept goes on a shelf of small pictures, the current one highlighted; Stays is greyed on a pairing already kept. **That's all** says "There's another." until every stable pairing is kept, and then waits for a new one. There are no slots and no count. Undo keeps the shelf.
- **Repair** (puzzles 3, 4, 7). Tap two places on one strip, or drag one onto the other, to swap them. Only one strip can differ from the printed strips: changing a second strip puts the first one back, and swapping back to the printed order clears the change. Stays is checked against the changed strips ("Can't stay."). The strip that differs is tinted, so a second change visibly puts the first one back. Can't is answered "One strip can do it." when one does. When none does, Can't asks for its reason: the board returns to the printed strips, Stays, Can't and the strips are put away, and "Tap two pairs that would both switch and share no letter." stays in view while the child taps circles and squares. A blocking pair is drawn as a pine dashed line; any other tap says who says no. The puzzle is solved when two of the tapped blocking pairs share no letter, captioned "One strip can change only one of these." The app never draws the reason for the child.
- **Design** (puzzles 6 and 11). Strips are live as in repair, with any number changed, and the pairing is changed by taps. The child keeps each pairing that can stay and presses That's all, so the design is checked by the child's own enumeration. Changing a strip clears the shelf. If That's all is right but the number is wrong, the board says "Only one can stay." or "Three can stay."; the puzzle is solved when exactly two are kept and That's all is right.
- **Last choices** (puzzle 12). Strips and pairing both change. It starts with A and X on their last choices, in different pairs, so they block and the start can't stay. Stays is checked twice: if the pairing can't stay it locks as above; if it can, but fewer than three letters have their partner at the far end of their strip, the board says "None get their last choice." (or One, Two). Letters with their last choice are named in their accessible labels.
- **Copy.** Each puzzle shows one objective: "Press Stays, or tap a circle and a square that would both rather be together." (check), "Change one strip so this pairing can stay." (repair), "Find every pairing that can stay.", "Change the strips so both pairings can stay.", "Change the strips so exactly two pairings can stay.", and "Make a pairing that can stay where three letters get their last choice." How to play gives the rules (what a strip is, what blocks, what can stay) and the controls.
- **Hints.** Before any move the first hint is the authored nudge. Then: in a check puzzle, the blocking pair in words ("A would rather have Y, and Y would rather have A.") or "Each wish to switch meets a no. Press Stays."; when locked, a blocking pair; in find every, the nearest pairing not yet kept ("Pair A with W."), then "Press Stays.", and "Press That's all." when none is left; in repair, the swap toward the nearest one-strip repair, keeping the strip the child is changing when it can still work ("On X's strip, swap A and C."), or, for puzzle 4, "A and Y would both switch, and so would B and X. No strip has a letter of each. Press Can't." and then the two pairs to tap; in design, swaps toward the nearest strips that work (a search outward from the child's strips, at most two swaps away with three pairs), then pairings and Stays; in last choices, swaps that bring each partner to its end of the strip and then the pairing, the fewest in all (five from the start). Hints are computed once per board and take a few milliseconds. The second level marks the letters, strip places or button on the board, and the third offers Apply hint. Hints alone finish every puzzle from the start and after a wrong move.

## The playground

Two, three or four pairs, chosen with three buttons showing a small pairing: two pairs where both circles have their first choice, puzzle 7's three-pair strips, and puzzle 10's two blocks. Strips and pairings change freely, Stays keeps a pairing that can stay (and locks on one that can't, as in the puzzles), and changing a strip clears the shelf. There is no goal, no That's all and no Hint, and it never counts as solved.

## Rules that keep the record honest

- The save is the claim (check), the one changed strip, the claim and the blocking pairs tapped after a right Can't (repair), or the strips when they change, the pairing, the pairings kept, the lock, the last claim and the answer to That's all (the others); the playground adds the size. Everything shown, including who says no, the shading and the certificate, is derived from it.
- Only legal moves are accepted: a pair of a circle and a square not already together; a claim only on a circle and a square not together, and in find every, last choices and the playground only while locked; no pairing, swap, Stays or That's all while locked, and no Undo out of a lock except of wrong claims; swaps of two different places on one strip, one strip at a time in repair, and after a right Can't only taps, each pair once; Stays not twice on one pairing; That's all only with something kept and not twice without a new pairing; nothing after a solve.
- Saved boards are checked: strips are rankings of the other side, the pairing is one to one, the repair strip differs from the printed one, every pairing kept can stay for the current strips and is kept once, a lock only on a pairing that can't stay and only until a blocking pair is tapped, That's all accepted only with every stable pairing kept, a Stays in last choices only on a pairing that can stay, the tapped reason for Can't only when no one strip works, on the printed strips, made of blocking pairs each tapped once, and a playground size that is a number.
- Blocking pairs, stable pairings, repairs and design witnesses are computed by `scripts/build-matching.mjs` with the mechanic's helpers and checked against the table above; `scripts/validate-matching.mjs` recomputes them by other methods.

## Not yet done

- **Children's play.** Nothing here has been tried by children, and the worksheets are unpiloted. Three things to watch: whether children read a strip from the letter outward (circles' strips go up, squares' go down) and ask both letters of a pair, or tap the first one-sided wish and learn from "X says no." and the rings; whether the lock after a wrong Stays is understood as "find the reason" or feels stuck, and whether children press Undo to get out of it and are puzzled that it does nothing (while held, Undo only takes back wrong claims), or reach for Restart and lose their shelf; and whether pairing by tapping a circle and a square, with the old partners pairing with each other, is predictable enough to search four-pair markets without losing track, or whether the shelf is enough of a record.
- **Deferred acceptance.** The card allows a version where the child plays the receivers (each square holds its best ask). Not built; the asking rules, the n² bound and proposer optimality stay on paper (4–5 P2–P5).
- **Missing worksheet tasks.** K–1 P4 (fill one blank strip so both letters stay, or show a blocker the blank can't touch), 4–5 P2's design "sides that disagree" and P3's keep-first run that ends unstable are not puzzles yet; the card lists them as design solves.
- **Rejected pairings.** A rejection needs a tapped blocker, but rejected pairings are not kept anywhere: a child can meet the same unstable pairing twice. If testers see that, a second shelf of rejected pairings with their blockers could help.
- **Story and grade levels.** Not on the Lantern Road; no keeper lines. Grade-free; not yet in a K–1, 2–3 or 4–5 trail.
