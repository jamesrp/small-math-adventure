# Footprint borders

October 17, 2026. Eleven puzzles and a playground built from Week 35 of the Bellingham math circle (frieze symmetry). They are in the puzzle satchel only, not on the Lantern Road. Nothing here has been played by children, and the Week 35 packet is unpiloted. The theme's [review card](https://github.com/jamesrp/math-circle-worksheets/blob/main/plans/review/week-35.md) says revise (for the worksheet's missing kit of footprint pieces, which doesn't hold up the port), and its App fit and Decision shaped this family: one cell tiled endlessly, so the kit, the tracing and the imagined ends drop away; the child places the F's four forms and drags, flips or turns a translucent copy that shows each footprint's match or miss; solves are builds with one move required and another forbidden, the shortest slide and glide, breaking a move while the border still repeats, and the forced half-turn centre; a "can't" is certified by one footprint the child points at. The F stays fixed, never edited, since an edited motif can gain symmetry. There are no "which move works?" quizzes and no snapping. The packet was not revised after the card.

## The mathematics

A border is one cell of P places in two rows, repeated forever along a strip. Each place holds nothing or one notched F, in one of four forms: as drawn (A), flipped across (V), flipped down (H) and half-turned (R). The F has no symmetry of its own (the validator checks its outline), so two Fs coincide only when they are in the same place and the same form.

- **The moves.** A move of the copy carries the strip onto itself: a slide along it, the flip over the middle line, a glide (the flip and a slide), a flip across a line straight across the strip, or a half-turn round a point of the middle line. In place units, x goes to x + u or to a − x, and y to ±y; every line and centre that can match sits on a whole or half place. A move matches when every F lands on an F of the form the move makes it. The forms compose like the moves' turning parts (the Klein four-group): a flip over the middle line turns A into H, a flip across into V, a half-turn into R.
- **One F decides.** Since the F has no symmetry, where one F lands decides a move. So one F that lands on a gap or a different F rules a move out. For the flip, or a slide by a given length either way, that F and the place it lands on are a certificate. For every half-turn at once, one F whose half-turned form the other row never has is a certificate, because every half-turn round a point of the middle line puts it in the other row, half-turned; for every flip across at once, one F whose flipped-across form its own row never has. These family certificates need no centre or line, which is why they rule out every point and every line (the guide's warning: failure round one chosen centre does not rule out every half-turn).
- **Shortest slides.** The matching slides are the multiples of the shortest one. A slide carries each F to one of the same form in its row, so if some F's nearest twin in its row is d places away, no slide shorter than d can match; when the slide by d matches, that F and its twin certify that d is shortest. The cell drawn need not be the shortest repeat (puzzle 2), and two close twins need not make a short slide (puzzle 11).
- **Shortest glides.** A glide done twice is a slide (the flip and a slide commute). So if the flip alone fails, every matching glide is (k + ½) times the shortest slide P, and the shortest glide is exactly P/2; if the flip matches, glides are the slides with a flip, and the shortest is P. A glide carries an F to the other row, flipped down, so the nearest such F certifies the shortest glide as for slides (puzzles 7, 9 and 11). Consequences the puzzles use: breaking the 2-place slide of a border that repeats every 4 forces any glide to be 2 places (puzzle 9), and a 1-place glide always brings a 2-place slide.
- **Two crossing flips force a half-turn.** The flip over the middle line takes (x, y) to (x, −y) and the flip across a crossing line takes (x, y) to (−x, y), measured from the crossing; one after the other they take (x, y) to (−x, −y), the half-turn round the crossing. Any border with both flips matches that half-turn untried, and others half a shortest slide apart (puzzle 10). The rule is for perpendicular lines; for other angles two flips make a turn by twice the angle.
- **Limits, and the rule that settles them.** The copy's moves are all the symmetries of a border with Fs in both rows: any symmetry keeps the two rows' heights, so it fixes the middle line (the validator checks there is none off the middle line or off the half-place grid). A border with Fs in one row only can have more, such as a half-turn round a point of that row's own line (AR/·· has one), which the copy can't show. So **Fs in both rows is a rule of the border**, stated in How to play's rules: while a row is empty, no claim can be made and nothing is solved, and the board says "A border needs an F in each row." once the copy has tried a move a Can't is about. Every claim is therefore judged where the copy's moves are every symmetry, and every "no half-turn" in the puzzles means round any point of the middle line, as the grown-up notes say. (The other choice, judging one-row borders round the occupied row's own line, would change puzzles 5 and 8's answers to 48 and 44 and need a second kind of half-turn; the rule keeps the worksheet's moves.) Every answer has Fs in both rows anyway, because each build requires a flip, a glide or a half-turn. There are seven kinds of frieze by which moves match; the puzzles don't need the list.

Experiments come first: drag the copy, press its buttons, put Fs down and watch them go red or green. The conjectures (a glide can work when the flip fails; the shortest glide is half the shortest slide; two crossing flips make a turn) come from what the copy shows. The explanations (one F decides; a glide twice is a slide; flips compose) are in each puzzle's grown-up notes, with the guide's witnesses T, G, H, R and E named where a puzzle uses one. Week 35's written work (4–5 Problem 3's "must a 6 cm slide match?" and Problem 6's complete symmetry list) stays on paper.

## How a puzzle plays

- **The board.** A paper strip with a dashed middle line; the cell is framed in leaf green with its repeats on either side, fading at the ends, 8 to 10 places in all. The border's Fs are sun yellow with ink outlines. The copy is drawn over them in translucent sky blue inside a dashed frame; each copy F is outlined pine where it lands on an F like it and red where it misses, so a match is a copy with no red. At home the copy is faint. Goal chips sit above the board, one per part: the move's picture (with its length, for a slide), crossed out for a move that must fail, with its Can't, Shortest or Turn here button. The copy's buttons (pictures of each move) and, where the cell can change, the four forms are below.
- **Moving the copy.** Drag along the board to slide it in whole places; the copy redraws as it goes and the slide is one move on release, up to one cell either way. The arrow keys slide it a place on the focused board. The buttons flip it over the middle line, flip it across, or turn it halfway round, in place, so a slide afterwards moves the line or the point: the flip then a slide is a glide. The copy remembers every kind of move it has made on this border (and how far each slide and glide went), and a move a goal asks for is ticked when the copy shows it.
- **Changing the cell.** Choose a form, then tap a place: it puts that F there, a tap with the same form takes it away, and taps on a repeat change the cell's place. In puzzles 4 and 9 the Fs are fixed in number: tap one to pick it up (pine ring) and tap an empty place to put it there. A change to the cell clears what the copy has shown, the moves it has tried and every claim made right; the copy stays where it is, and counts as an attempt only if it matches the new cell (then that match is ticked), so a Can't after an edit needs the move tried again. Refusals are not cleared (below).
- **Can't for one move (the flip, the 2-place slide).** Can't is off until the copy has made that move on this border, and while a row is empty. After Can't the copy is hidden and every place in the window is a target, unmarked, in row order (the top row left to right, then the bottom), so the next target after an F is beside it, never where the flip puts it: tap an F, then the place the move puts it. Any place can be tapped first, a gap too, and the claim is judged when the second tap is in. Right when the move puts it there and there is no F like it there (a gap or a different F): the F is ringed pine, the place red, and the copy's one F is drawn landing on it. Wrong: "There's no F there." (the first tap was a gap), "It doesn't land there." (the move doesn't take it there) or "It lands on one like it." The taps clear and Can't stays off.
- **Refusals stay with their border.** A refusal is saved with the cell it was made on (and the copy's place, for a Shortest). Whenever the board is that again, after Undo or after changing the cell and changing it back, the claim stays off and the board says "A claim was refused on this border." (or "Shortest was refused with the copy here."). Edits, copy moves and Undo never clear one. Restart does, at the price of the whole attempt, so a fresh try at a refused claim costs a rebuild; on a build the other way on is another border that works.
- **Can't for every half-turn or flip across.** Can't is off until the copy has made that kind of move. After Can't, tap a place; the four forms become the answer buttons, all alike; tap the form its F would become. Right when it is the F's half-turned (or flipped-across) form and the row it must land in (the other row for a half-turn, its own for a flip across) has none: the F is ringed and the row tinted. Wrong, whatever was wrong (a gap, the F or the form): "That doesn't rule out every half-turn." (or "every flip across"), one text, so a refusal doesn't say which part to change, and never says the claim is false.
- **Shortest.** Shortest is on only while the copy matches by that kind of move (a slide, or the flip and a slide). Tap a place, then the nearest F it could land on: its twin in its row for a slide, its flipped-down form in the other row for a glide. Right when it is that kind, no such F is nearer, and it is as far as the copy went: the pair is ringed with a line between. Wrong: "There's no F there.", "It can't land there." (a gap or the wrong kind of F), "There's a nearer one." or "That one is nearer than the copy went." The refusal stays with the copy where it was: slid away and back, Shortest is off again; slid the same distance the other way, it is on. With both ways refused the hint says to Restart.
- **Turn here (puzzle 10).** On once both flips have been shown. Every half place along the middle line is drawn as a dot, so the crossing is one choice among fifteen, and a tap goes to the nearest. Right when a half-turn round that point matches, wherever it is (on a border that repeats every 2 places, every place along works): the turned copy is drawn, all pine, round a dot. Wrong: the turned copy with its misses and a red dot, "That turn doesn't match.", kept with the border as above. There is no Turn button; pressing Flip and then Flip across turns the copy halfway, which is the experiment the theorem comes from, and the status line then says "flipped over the middle line and flipped across", never "turned".
- **Copy.** One objective per puzzle, such as "Make a border that matches after a glide but not after the flip alone." How to play opens with what a border and a match are and, where a puzzle has one, what a glide is ("A glide is the flip over the middle line and then a slide."), then the gestures and the claims; the rules, including Fs in both rows, are in its details. The buttons are named the same way everywhere: Flip (over the middle line), Flip across, Turn.
- **Hints.** Before any move, the first hint is the authored nudge; it is never a claim. Then hints change the cell toward the nearest answer, one F at a time ("Put an as-drawn F at place 1 of the top row.", "Move the flipped-down F at place 2 of the bottom row to place 3 of the bottom row."), avoiding every border with a refused claim; then move the copy to show each required move (bringing it back to as drawn first, then "Flip the copy over the middle line.", "Slide the copy 1 place left."); then, for each claim, the attempt ("Try the flip alone: flip the copy over the middle line."), then the reason with the claim ("Flipped over the middle line, the as-drawn F at place 1 of the top row lands on a gap. Press Can't, then tap that F and where it lands.", "The nearest other as-drawn F in its row is 2 places from the one at place 1 of the top row. Press Shortest, then tap those two Fs.", "Two flips that cross make a half-turn round the point where they cross. Press Turn here and tap that point."). The second level rings the places, the copy's target frame, the point or the button; the third offers Apply hint. Hints alone finish every puzzle from a fresh start, after wrong edits and copy moves, after a refused claim, after going away and back, after Undo and after Restart. When every way left is refused (a Shortest refused both ways, or every border that works refused), the hint is a note, "Every way left to finish has a refused claim. Restart to try again.", with nothing to apply; it is not a dead end, whose rescue Undo would take refusals back. Hints are memoised per board, and each takes well under 100 ms of CPU time.

## Certificates

The child points at every certificate except a match, whose certificate is the copy itself, all pine. Each can fail, and the validator counts how many of all possible tap pairs (any place first, gaps too, then any other place or a form) pass on the board where the hints make the claim: the flip 8 of 240 (puzzles 3, 4, 5, 8 and 9), the 2-place slide 12 of 240, shortest slides 18 of 240 (puzzle 2), 8 of 240 (7) and 4 of 380 (11), shortest glides 12 of 240 and 6 of 380, the family claims 8 of 64, Turn here 3 of 15.

Taps down the page fail too. The validator renders each claim and takes its targets in page order: the first F, then the next target (the next F, for a Shortest; the first form, for a family claim), and keyboard order, Enter on the first target and then the next. Over every answer of each build and on each fixed border, neither ever passes the flip, the 2-place slide, Shortest or Turn here. A family claim's first form is the right one on some borders, at chance: page order 5 of 20 answers in puzzle 6 and 9 of 36 in puzzle 8, keyboard order 4 of 20 and 7 of 36. Any fixed order of the four forms does that well on a quarter of the answers, since the answers are the same with every form relabelled; it is a guess, and a wrong one locks the claim on that border.

For a family claim the place is not the main choice, the form is (which F does a half-turn or a flip across make of this one?). A wrong pick locks the claim on that border through edits and Undo, with one text whatever was wrong, so trying the forms in turn costs a Restart and a rebuild, or another border that works, for each. Turn here is a claim about where the move goes, not a "can't"; the crossing is drawn among fifteen dots, any matching centre is accepted, and no Turn button is offered, so the half-turn comes from the two flips (pressing one and then the other shows it).

## The playground

A cell of 2 to 6 places (the number buttons; a shorter cell keeps the first places), all three buttons, free Fs, and the copy. No goals, no claims, no Hint; How to play's grown-up notes suggest the guide's witnesses T, G, H, R and E to build.

## Rules that keep the record honest

- The save is the cell, the copy's state (which way it faces and where its frame is), the moves it has made on this cell (each kind, each slide's and glide's length, and the flip across the crossing line), the parts it has shown, the claims judged right with their taps, the claims refused with their taps, reason and board (the cell, and the copy for a Shortest), in order, at most 64 (the oldest dropped first), and which claim was judged last. Everything drawn is derived from it.
- **Undo takes back moves, not refusals.** Undo puts back the board before, with every refusal made since. A refusal locks its claim whenever its board comes back, after Undo, an edit and its reverse, or a slide and back; only Restart clears refusals. Undo after a refusal takes away its words (the note that the claim was refused here takes their place), so the board visibly changes.
- Only legal moves are accepted: slides by a whole, nonzero number of places within a cell of home; only the puzzle's buttons; places in the cell; F moves onto empty places only, keeping the same Fs; claims only for a part with that kind of claim, after the attempt, with Fs in both rows, not twice, not while a refusal stands on this board; taps on places in the board's window (any place first, then a different place or a form); Turn here only after every required move is shown; nothing after a solve, and no solve while a row is empty.
- Saved boards are checked against all of this: the cell's length and forms (and, with moving Fs, the same Fs; with a fixed border, that border), the copy's range, the copy's own attempt on record (always on a fixed border; on a build, whenever it matches), each part shown only where it can be and after its own move was tried, every match the copy makes now recorded, every right claim judged again with its attempt (a Shortest with the copy's slide or glide by the shortest length on record), no claim with a row empty, every refusal judged again on its own board to the same reason, and the last claim. The October 17 review's forged saves (a Shortest done with the copy never moved, a glide shown with only the flip tried, both flips shown and Turn here done with nothing tried) are refused.
- The answers are computed by `scripts/build-friezes.mjs` with the mechanic's own functions (forms by XOR, a search over every cell or arrangement, or over cells the required flips fix) and checked again by `scripts/validate-friezes.mjs` with plane isometries on placed Fs.

## Not yet done

- **Children's play.** Nothing here has been tried by children, and the worksheet is unpiloted. Three things to watch: whether children read the copy's red and pine outlines over the yellow Fs as "misses" and "matches" without help, and drag to make the red go away rather than predicting; whether the Can't taps (an F, then where the move puts it) make sense as "this one has nowhere to go", or children tap pairs at random (a refusal locks the claim on that border until Restart, which may frustrate, and a family refusal doesn't say whether the F or the form was wrong); and whether the four form buttons, used both to place Fs and to answer a family claim, are clear, and whether children can tell flipped-across from half-turned at this size (the notch and the arrow are the only cues).
- **Drag on a phone.** The board takes horizontal drags and leaves vertical ones to the page; whether a child's sideways swipe sometimes scrolls instead is untested on real phones.
- **One-row borders.** Settled by the rule that a border has Fs in both rows: no claim is judged and nothing is solved while a row is empty (see Limits). Whether children find the rule natural, or are puzzled when Can't stays off with every F in one row, is untested.
- **Small targets on puzzle 11.** Its 6-place cell needs 10 places on the board (a repeat of 2 places each side, so the flipped-across F's twin 6 places along can be tapped), so on a 375 px phone each place is about 30 px wide (36 px tall). Taps go to the place under the finger, and nothing between places is lost, but small fingers may need care; a wider board would scroll sideways.
- **Paper only.** 4–5 Problem 3 (must a 6 cm slide match?) and Problem 6 (every move of a border, and why no other matches) stay on paper, in the grown-up notes of puzzles 7, 9 and 10. K–1 Problem 7 (trading borders) is the playground.
- **Story.** Not on the Lantern Road; no keeper lines.
- **Grade levels.** Grade-free; not yet in a K–1, 2–3 or 4–5 trail. The card's K–1 route maps to puzzles 1 to 6.
