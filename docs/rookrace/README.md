## The mathematics

A token sits on a square board with a star in the bottom-left corner. A turn slides it straight left or straight down, one or more squares, and whoever moves it onto the star wins. On the queen boards a turn may also slide it diagonally down and to the left. With piles, a turn takes one or more pebbles from one pile, and whoever takes the last pebble wins.

- **Every start is a first-player or a second-player win,** and working back from the end sorts them: a start is a second-player win exactly when every move from it reaches a first-player win. No move joins two second-player starts, and from every other start some move reaches one.
- **The rook board is two piles.** The square x columns right of the star and y rows up is the game with piles x and y. Its second-player squares are the diagonal x = y: every move leaves the diagonal, and from any other square one move returns to it. Going second from the diagonal, copying keeps the token there until it reaches the star.
- **The queen board is Wythoff's game.** Each row, column and down-left diagonal holds at most one second-player square, since a move would join two. They are (1, 2), (3, 5), (4, 7), (6, 10), (8, 13), … and their mirror images: the pairs (⌊kφ⌋, ⌊kφ²⌋), whose k-th difference is k. The smaller numbers and the larger numbers together list every positive whole number once (Beatty).
- **Three piles: Bouton's theorem.** Split each pile into bundles of 1, 2, 4, 8, …, each size at most once in a pile. The second player wins exactly when every bundle size appears an even number of times across the piles (the Nim-sum is 0). A move always changes one pile and so breaks a pair; from an unpaired position, the pile holding the largest unpaired bundle can be rebuilt to pair everything again. So two piles fix the third: with piles below 8 the second-player starts are a, b and a ⊕ b. With piles of 1 to 3 the only one is 1, 2, 3; with 1 to 6 there are four; with 1 to 7, seven.

What the app checks: in a race, that the child chooses who starts and then wins against a perfect opponent, three games in a row from new starts; in Every start, that the marked squares or kept starts are exactly the second-player starts. Explanations (why copying works, why the bundle rule holds) stay with grown-ups, in the notes.

What a child is meant to discover: on the rook board, that the diagonal is where to hand over the token, and that copying keeps it there; on the queen board, that the safe squares come in mirror pairs that no move joins; with three piles, which starts lose for the player to move, and (in grades 4–5) a rule about bundles that the app never states.

## How a puzzle plays

- **The board.** Squares on paper with ink lines, the star in sun in the bottom-left corner, and the token in pine. A small key beside the board draws a token with an arrow for each direction it may slide (two for the rook, three for the queen), so the rule is on the screen before the first game. The board is at most 380 px wide, and on a short screen it shrinks (to `100svh − 430px`, not below 220 px) so that the buttons under it are in view without scrolling on a 375 × 667 phone, even when the top bar wraps onto two lines. It never shrinks below 27 px a column, though: the 11-by-11 board stays 297 px there, squares of about 25 px between the lines, where the plain rule would make them 20 px. With a short name every state of every board fits 375 × 667; when a longer name puts the top bar on two lines, the 11-by-11 board is a short scroll (see Not yet done).
- **Star race.** Before each game, **Go first** or **Go second**, styled alike so neither looks like the default. On the child's turn every square the token can reach carries a small dot and is a button; a tap anywhere else says the move is not allowed. I reply at once, never missing a winning move (from a losing square I leave the fewest winning replies, then the longest game). A dashed ring marks the square my token left, and one line says what I did ("I moved 2 left. Your turn"). Three pips fill with wins in a row; a loss empties them. After a loss, **Same start again** replays that start as practice that does not count; **New start** draws a new one, half the time from the second-player squares and never the start just played. On the 4-by-4 and 5-by-5 boards a start one move from the star is never drawn: on 4 by 4 those are half the first-player squares, each won in one tap whoever understands the game. There is no Undo: the choice of who starts is final, and a wrong choice loses against perfect play.
- **Every start on a board.** Tap a square to mark it (pine) and again to clear it. **Play**, then a tap on any square, then Go first or Go second, plays a practice game from that square against me (the worksheet's "play many games on this board, starting on any square"); Back returns to the marks, untouched. **That's all** judges every mark at once. While a second-player square is missing, it says "There is another." and stays off until the marks change, whatever else is marked. Once none is missing, That's all is final: if a marked square is one where whoever starts can win, I play it at once. The square gets an ochre ring, the line above the board begins "Whoever starts from the ringed square can win. I go first." before my move, and I go first and win against any reply (there is no Back until the game ends). When I win, the marks are cleared ("I reached the star. The marks are cleared.", one line, so Back stays in view), and Back returns to an empty board. No count is ever shown.
- **Every start with piles.** Three columns of slots set the three piles (tap the third slot to make a pile of 3). **Keep** puts the start on the shelf as a chip, sorted, since the order of the piles does not matter; keeping it again says "Already on the shelf."; tapping a chip takes it off. **Play** tries the start being built against me, choosing who starts, with the duel's pebbles (tap a pebble to lift it and those above, then Take). That's all judges the shelf as on a board: "There is another." while a start is missing; otherwise a wrong start ("Whoever starts from 2, 2 and 5 can win. I go first.") is played out at once, and when I take the last pebble the shelf is cleared. Piles of 1 to 3, with its single answer, is the exception: there a wrong start is played out even while the answer is missing, and "There is another." never comes (see Certificates). How to play says which: on the five puzzles with several answers, "While a start is still missing, That's all says “There is another.”" and "Once none is missing, That's all is final: …"; on piles of 1 to 3 only "That's all is final: …". The line about That's all or Keep sits just under the buttons, above the shelf, which can grow long.
- **Hints.** In a race, Hint shows the puzzle's three authored nudges, one per press, and never who should start or a move: the first suggests Same start again after a loss, the second rings (in ochre, on the board) the starts the child lost after going first, the third asks where my moves left the token. They show after a loss too, when Same start again is on the screen; only after a win does Hint say "Try a new start." (Duels 1–3 in Proofs now do the same). In Every start, the child sees the puzzle's first authored hint, before any tap. After that, Hint names the next step toward the answer: take off a wrong mark first ("One marked square is a square where whoever starts can win.", highlighted on the board from the second press), then add a missing start (on the piles one pile at a time, then Keep), then That's all; Apply hint can play it. During a play-out it says any move will do. So Hint, unlike That's all, does tell a child whether a mark is wrong, as hints do in Fair shares. The puzzle's second and third authored hints are for grown-ups: the notes list them as starting-board hints, as for every family whose hints are computed from the board.

## Certificates and claims

- **Star race.** The certificate is three wins in a row against perfect play from new starts. Starts are drawn half from second-player squares, so always choosing Go first passes about 1 time in 8 (the validator measures 12.5–15%). The save keeps each won game of the streak (its start, who went first and every turn), and validation replays each against the perfect opponent, so a full streak cannot be saved without three legal wins.
- **Why Star race hints alone do not reach a solve.** Port-common asks that hints alone reach a solve; Star race is waived, like the Proofs duels it is built on. It is an evidence round: the decision is who starts, and a hint that said so (or played the move) would make the three wins certify nothing. Its hints are the three authored nudges, by level. The same boards do have hint chains that reach a solve: Every start 1 is the rook board, and Every start 4 and 6 are the queen boards, where hints alone mark every second-player square. The validator plays every race with a solver instead.
- **Every start.** A find-every claim judged as a whole, as port-common asks: no per-start check, no slots per answer, no found-count. Once nothing is missing, That's all is final, as the card's "the app plays the side a wrong mark gives away, and wins" asks. With several answers it answers in this order:
  1. If a second-player start is missing: "There is another." (port-common's words), and That's all stays off until the marks or the shelf change.
  2. Otherwise, if a marked start is one where the first player can win: I play it at once, going first, and win against any reply ("This one doesn't …" in this game's form). When the game ends, the marks or the shelf are cleared.
  3. Otherwise: solved.

  With a single answer (piles of 1 to 3, and only there) steps 1 and 2 swap: a wrong start is played out first, so "There is another." never comes.

  Why missing comes first: asked the other way round, one mark and That's all would be a yes-or-no check on that start, a tap or a lost game each. Now a That's all with a few marks says only "There is another.", and a wrong start is named only to a shelf that holds every answer, and costs that whole shelf. The validator plays the guessing strategies against the mechanic and counts taps (a pile take is two; "lost" counts the games I win going first):

  | Puzzle | Knowing the answer | Playing every start (second, perfectly) | Mark everything, strip: before → now | One start at a time: before → now | Leave one out (now) |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | Every start 1 | 6 | 201 | 96 → 701 (30 lost) | 111 → no information | 664 (29 lost) |
  | Every start 2 | 4 | 86 | 41 → 178 (9 lost) | 19 → 27 (4 lost; see below) | 146 (8 lost) |
  | Every start 3 | 13 | 669 | 234 → 4,184 | 255 → no information | 4,049 |
  | Every start 4 | 7 | 325 | 178 → 2,154 | 196 → no information | 2,090 |
  | Every start 5 | 22 | 1,071 | 347 → 9,048 | 383 → no information | 8,854 |
  | Every start 6 | 9 | 660 | 345 → 7,542 | 369 → no information | 7,419 |

  "Before" is the first design, where a wrong start was named first and rung, and one tap took it off. "Leave one out" (keep every start but one, so "There is another." means that one is an answer) is the cheapest test of a single start left; like marking everything, it now costs more than playing every start in a practice game, and 10 to 400 times knowing the answer.

  Why one answer is different: on piles of 1 to 3 (one answer among ten starts) a guess at one start is a guess at the whole answer, and with missing first a wrong guess alone got "There is another." for nothing: a blind guesser, keeping one start at a time in a random order, solved it in about 26 taps without losing a game. With the wrong start first, each wrong guess is a game I win going first, and the shelf is cleared. Over 200 shuffled orders a blind guesser now spends about 37 taps and loses 4.5 games on average (at worst 74 taps and 9 games), exactly the games Play loses from those starts; testing the same starts with Play until one is won costs about 52 taps. Guessing cannot cost much more than play here, since a wrong guess is the very game Play gives and the guesser saves only the winning game at the end; what it no longer gets is a free answer. The validator checks, order by order, that every wrong guess is a lost game and that Play loses the same ones.
- **The certificate of completeness** is the whole set, so there is nothing smaller to tap: the child's own marks are the argument (each marked start's moves all reach unmarked starts), and the app judges it. That's all with nothing marked does nothing. Random shelves almost never pass: keeping each start with chance ½ passes 1 time in 1,000 on piles of 1 to 3 and never in 20,000 tries elsewhere; matching the answer count, 4% on piles of 1 to 3 (one start in ten) and 0 elsewhere.
- **Nothing gives the answer away.** Titles name the board or the piles, How to play states the moves and controls, and first hints point at play. No visible line counts anything except the race's three wins. The grown-ups' notes, which hold the answers, are left out of How to play on these 11 puzzles (`"proof": true`, as the Proofs duels do through `band`); the Grown-ups catalog still lists them. Nothing else reads `proof` except the road's copies of Proofs puzzles, and these puzzles are not on the road.

## Changes to Pebble Duel

- **The bundle table is earned.** How to play on the 12 Pebble Duel puzzles no longer shows the "Explore … bundles" table until Duel 1 (Proofs) is solved, and the table no longer says "A balanced position has an even count in every column" (`nim.help` in `dist/deduction.js`, which now takes `ctx.completed`; `dist/main.js` passes it).
- **No rule sentence.** The move hint ("Take 2 from pile 3, leaving 1, 4 and 5.") drops "Keep returning the opponent to a balanced position." Duel 2's hints no longer state the bundle rule; they point at the child's lost starts ("Lost a game? Same start again replays those piles…", "Think of the starts you lost after going first…"). Duel 1's first hint ("Are they equal?") named the deciding condition and now points at lost starts too; its second and third hints (copying) are unchanged. After a lost duel, Hint now shows these nudges instead of "Try a new start." (`duelHint` in `dist/proofs.js`), so the first one appears while Same start again is on the screen.
- **Titles by start.** "Leave the pair", "Build a mirror", … "Rebundle across five piles" become "Piles of 1 and 2", "Piles of 2 and 5", … "Piles of 3, 5, 8, 10 and 12". First hints that named the winning move or said that several exist ("Leave the other player two piles of 1.", "Try reducing 8 to 1…", "Several piles can repair the imbalance.") now point at play or at bundles without the move or the rule. Three that still pointed at the winning pile after the first rewrite now point at the child's own lost games or at the position: puzzle 6 ("When the opponent wins, Undo and look at what it left you each time."), puzzle 8 ("Which piles would you like to leave the opponent?") and puzzle 10 ("When the opponent won, what piles did it leave you?"). Puzzle 12's "Which piles have an 8?" names three piles, each of which has a winning move; it stays.
- **The two-pile duel comes early.** Next after puzzle 2 opens Duel 1, and Next after Duel 1 opens puzzle 3 (a `next` field on the two puzzles, honoured by `completionCard` in `dist/ui.js`). Why this way: Duel 1 already sits first in the satchel (Proofs is above the levels), so a child working through Next from puzzle 1 was the one who never met it. Renumbering Pebble Duel would break the Lantern Road and caravan, which pick Pebble Duel puzzles by number (ridge duels use 2, 4 and 5), and old saves; moving Duel 1 out of Proofs would change its numbering, its road copy (the Fair final uses Duels 1–3 as proofs) and the Proofs tests. Proofs keeps working: Duel 1 is still Proofs 01, and the proofs list order is unchanged. One change a Proofs player will see: Next after Duel 1 opens Pebble Duel puzzle 3, not Duel 2, whichever list it was opened from (the completion card does not know). That suits Duel 2, whose prerequisites are Pebble Duel's easy and medium puzzles.
- **How to play** for Pebble Duel opens with the move: "A turn takes one or more pebbles from one pile."

Puzzle ids, numbers and revisions are unchanged, so saves and the road keep working.

## Rules that keep the record honest

- Only moves the rules allow are accepted: a reachable square on the child's turn; a choice of who starts only before a game (and, in a practice game on a board, only after a start is tapped); Same start again only after a loss; nothing after a solve. On the shelf, pile sizes 1 to the puzzle's largest; That's all only with something kept and no standing "There is another."; Back from a play-out only after it ends.
- Saves are replayed from their start: every turn alternates players, every move is legal, and every one of my moves from a winning position wins, so a saved game is one I really played. Saves land before a choice, on the child's turn, or after a game. A race's streak equals its list of won games; a loss empties it; practice games never add to it; a full streak ends on a fresh win. An Every start save keeps the marks or the shelf sorted and distinct; "There is another." must be exactly what That's all says about them; a play-out must start from a first-player start that That's all named about the marks it holds, and once it ends (with my win) the marks must be cleared; done only when they are right.

## Not yet done

- **Children's play.** Nothing here has been tried by children. Things to watch first:
  1. Whether children choose who starts by looking at the token's square, or fall back on Go first or on who won last time (the card's prediction for kindergartners).
  2. Whether the move key and the dots show the queen's diagonal well enough before the first game, and whether the boards' squares are easy to tap on a small phone: boards up to 8 by 8 fill a phone's width with squares of 40 px or more, but the 11-by-11 board's squares are about 30 px on 375 × 667 and 32 px on a 390 × 844 phone (40 px on an upright tablet): eleven 40 px squares do not fit across a phone.
  3. Whether children decide starts by playing them (Play, Same start again) or mark a guess and press That's all. A wrong That's all now costs a lost game and every mark; watch whether that teaches or only discourages, and whether "There is another." while a wrong mark sits on the board misleads anyone. On piles of 1 to 3 each wrong guess is a lost game: watch whether children then switch to Play.
  4. Whether Go first and Go second, now styled alike, change how often children choose to go second.
- **The bridge.** The board and the piles are never shown side by side; doing so would give away grades 2–3 Problem 5. A puzzle that asks for the two piles matching a square could come later.
- **Explanations.** "Explain how you can always win" (2–3 P3, 4–5 P7) and "how you know you have found them all" stay with grown-ups.
- **Duel 3** still has a third hint that states the bundle rule; Pebble Duel's own hard puzzles still hint at bundles (without the rule). Pebble Duel's own opponent, when it is losing, still moves at random (the card's note); the new groups' opponent does not.
- **A streak's starts are not proved random.** A hand-made save could hold three real wins from easy starts; only the wins themselves are checked.
- **Story.** The groups are not on the Lantern Road. A Star race could be a ridge stop's side puzzle.
- **The road's Fair final does not unlock the bundle table.** The table in Pebble Duel's How to play waits for Duel 1 solved in Proofs; winning Duels 1–3 as the Fair final's road copies does not count, since those are separate puzzles.
- **The 11-by-11 board on a small phone.** The boards run to the panel's edges on a phone; on 375 × 667 the 11-by-11 board's squares are about 30 px, and with the one-row top bar every state keeps its controls (and, in a play-out, its bottom row) in view (the smoke checks each). Larger squares would need a zoomable board.
- **The 20-by-20 queen board** (4–5 P9) is not built; it would need a zoomable board.
