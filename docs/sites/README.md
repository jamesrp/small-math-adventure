# Nearest dots

October 10, 2026. Twelve puzzles and a playground built from Week 15 of the Bellingham math circle (nearest-site regions). They are in the puzzle satchel only, not on the Lantern Road. Nothing here has been played by children. The Week 15 worksheet was piloted at home, where the organizer tried the core maps by hand with three children, but not yet in the circle. The theme's [review card](https://github.com/jamesrp/math-circle-worksheets/blob/main/plans/review/week-15.md) says keep, and its decision of October 10 shaped this family: a new family of nearest-site regions with dots on a half-square grid so that every edge is exact; make a target region within a dot budget, touch some regions and avoid another, and take a place while keeping others; the child's segment through R as the certificate for "can't", and a checked "can't with fewer" beside each fewest-dots witness; regions hidden until Check, and no question asking which dot owns a place.

## The mathematics

A **place** belongs to every dot nearest to it, measured by straight-line distance; a place just as near to two or more dots belongs to all of them. A dot's **region** is the closed set of its places (its Voronoi cell). Everything below is for dots at distinct points of the plane.

- **Two dots: the halfway line.** The places just as near to s as to k form the perpendicular bisector of s and k, and the places at least as near to s form the closed half-plane 2(k − s)·z ≤ |k|² − |s|². So a region is the intersection of one half-plane for each other dot: it contains its dot, has **no dents** (it is convex), has straight sides, and may run on forever. Each side lies on the halfway line between the region's dot and one other dot. Two regions **touch** when they share a place, even a single point; a corner where three or more regions meet is a place three or more dots share, the centre of a circle through those dots with no dot inside. Adding a dot adds one more half-plane to every old region, so it can only take places away.
- **Parting two regions (puzzle 2).** Two regions touch exactly when some circle through their two dots has no dot strictly inside (its centre is then a place both share). With A and B alone they share their whole halfway line. One more dot C parts them exactly when C lies strictly between A and B on their segment: every circle through A and B holds the open segment inside it, so C is inside each one; and if C is anywhere else, some circle through A and B misses it (one with its centre far out on the side away from C, or the circle on AB as diameter when C is on their line but outside the segment). For A (−2, 0) and B (2, 0) that is the 7 grid points between them. Two dots off the segment can part them too, for example 1 square above and 1 square below the middle.
- **Making a region (the mirror construction).** Let T be a region with straight sides and no dents, holding the dot A inside. Put a new dot at A's mirror image across each side of T. Each side is then the halfway line between A and its new dot, so A's region is exactly T. Conversely, each side of A's region lies on the halfway line between A and some other dot, and a line is the halfway line between A and only one point, A's mirror image across it. So when T has k sides and no other dot is present, the k mirror images are needed, and with a budget of k they are the only answer (puzzles 1, 3, 5 and 10). A spare slot can hold a dot that changes nothing: an extra dot adds one half-plane, so a set of extras changes nothing exactly when each one alone does. In puzzle 4 (the corner x ≤ 1, y ≤ 1, four slots) an extra dot z changes nothing exactly when it is not left of A or below it and is at least as far from the corner (1, 1) as A is, since its half-plane must hold the whole quarter-plane; 25 free grid points do, so 1 + 25 + 300 = 326 placements work, all holding the two mirror images. On the half-square grid a mirror image is not always a grid point: the worksheet's triangle in grades 4–5 Problem 7 needs dots at (±24/13, 16/13), so puzzle 10 uses a triangle whose mirror images land on the grid.
- **Taking a place (no dents).** If two places belong to A, so does every place on the segment between them, because each half-plane holds the whole segment. So when R lies on the segment between two places A must keep (A itself counts), no dots can take R from A: that segment is the certificate (puzzle 11). More generally A's region holds the whole shape spanned by A and the places it keeps (a segment or a triangle here). A place outside that shape can be cut off from it by a line, and a dot at A's mirror image across the line takes the place and leaves the shape to A (puzzles 8 and 12); in the app that dot must also land on the grid inside the frame, which the answers below check. The puzzles use only segments as certificates; a place inside the triangle A, P, Q but off its sides would need the triangle, and no puzzle asks for it.
- **Closing a region (the fewest dots).** A region is closed in (bounded) only if it has at least three sides: one half-plane runs off, two give a strip, a wedge or a smaller half-plane, all running off. Each side needs its own other dot, so A alone needs at least three new dots, and three around A close it (puzzle 9). The app also checks every placement of fewer dots on its grid. (B between A and C on a line already has the two parallel sides of a strip, and one more half-plane cuts the strip off at one end only, so B needs two new dots: the worksheet's grades 4–5 Problem 6. Its puzzle was dropped in review to make room for puzzle 2; see Not yet done.)
- **A place for four.** Four dots on one circle with no dot inside share its centre. With A, B and C each 2 squares from the middle of the board (grades 2–3 Problem 2), the only free grid point 2 squares from the middle is opposite C (puzzle 6). Each corner of the frame is 3√2 squares (over 4) from the middle, so with D in a corner A, B and C still share the middle and a Check shows it, which puzzle 6's first hint uses. The same place counts as touching: in puzzle 7, D there touches C at that single point. D's region never touches only one of A, B and C: outside the triangle ABC or on its sides, D is on the boundary of the hull of the four dots and borders its two neighbours along it; strictly inside, D's region is closed and has at least three sides, each shared with one of A, B and C. The validator checks every grid point.

**What children are meant to discover.** Experiments come first: put dots down, press Check, and see the regions. The conjectures are that the edge is halfway between two dots, that only a dot between two others parts their regions, that a new dot goes as far past an edge as the region's dot is before it (a mirror image), that a place between two kept places can't be taken, and that a closed region needs three dots around it (or two more for a dot between two others). The explanations (equal distances on the halfway line, a region has no dents, a closed shape needs three sides) are grown-up conversations in each puzzle's notes. The worksheet's written explanations stay on paper.

**Grade bands.** The puzzles are grade-free. As a rough map: puzzles 1–4 need no reading with a grown-up reading the goal and match the K–1 packet (two dots and a third, Problems 2 and 7; the strip, Problem 8) and grades 2–3 Problem 8's corner; 5–8 match grades 2–3 (a slanted edge, a place for four, touch and avoid) and the first place to take from grades 4–5 Problem 8; 9–12 match grades 4–5 (fewest dots, the triangle, taking a place or showing it can't be taken). Easy, Medium and Hard have four puzzles each. The playground fits every band.

These are **Voronoi diagrams** (David M. Mount, *CMSC 754 Computational Geometry*, Lecture 10, pp. 1–3: the definition, cells as intersections of half-planes, convexity, and vertices as centres of empty circles).

## How a puzzle plays

- **The board.** Graph paper with the frame drawn in ink, whole-square lines in a clear grey-green and half-square lines faint, so squares can be counted; the board shows half a square of shaded margin past the frame (a quarter square on a phone), and regions are drawn out to the board's edge, so a region that runs on past the frame is seen running off (the map is cropped, not panned). Fixed dots are discs in sun, sky and rose with their letter; new dots are small ink discs (D in puzzles 6 and 7 is labelled D). Marked places in the take puzzles are crosses with an italic letter. In the make puzzles the target is shaded with a dashed edge.
- **Moves.** Tap where grid lines cross inside the frame to put a dot there; tap a dot to take it away; drag a dot to move it (one move, one Undo). A drag can start up to two fifths of a square from a dot, so a dot is a 40 px target to drag on a phone; a tap still goes to the nearest point, so a tap beside a dot puts a new dot there rather than taking the old one away. A letter sits in the little square up and right of its point, and a tap on the letter goes to that point, never to the crossing beside it: on A it does nothing, on D it takes D away, on R it puts a dot on R. On a touch screen a dragged dot rides one square above the finger, ringed in pine with an ochre cross through its crossing, so the finger never hides where it will land. Phone taps don't flash the board. Slots under the board show how many new dots are left, and a tap when they are full shakes them instead of adding a dot. A mouse shows a ring where a dot would go. With a keyboard, the arrow keys move a ring, Enter or Space puts down, picks up or drops a dot (the first press only shows the ring), Delete or Backspace takes one away, and Escape drops a dot held.
- **Check.** Nothing about the regions is shown until **Check**: then every region is tinted (A, B, C in their colours, D in leaf green, other new dots grey), every edge is drawn in ink, a small ring marks each place three dots share and a larger pine ring each place four share. The region the puzzle is about is outlined in pine when it is right and in ink when not; in puzzle 2 the edge A and B still share is drawn in ochre, and in puzzle 7 a contact with C's region; in the take puzzles each marked place gets a pine ring when it is right and a dashed ochre ring when not. Any change (a dot put down, moved or taken away) hides the regions again, and they are hidden while a dot is dragged, so a dot can't be wiggled until an edge lines up. The make, part, touch, four and take goals are solved at the Check that shows them right.
- **Can't** (take puzzles). Can't is greyed until there are dots on the board and Check has shown them: a claim is drawn round the child's own try, and a fresh board's Can't does nothing. Press Can't (it stays pressed) and tap two of A and the places A keeps; rings show where to tap (with a keyboard, Tab reaches them and Enter or Space taps). If the place to take is on the segment between them, the puzzle is solved: the segment is drawn in pine over the try's map, with R ringed. Otherwise the segment is drawn dashed in ochre with "Misses R.", and the same pair waits until something changes. A refusal says only whether R is on that segment, which the board already shows, so it is not a yes/no oracle about the puzzle and needs no lock; a right claim always needs the certificate.
- **Fewest** (fit puzzles). Fewest is greyed until Check shows the region inside the frame. With the fewest dots it solves the puzzle and draws the argument: each side's whole halfway line, dashed in pine, and each side's dot joined to the region's dot, so the sides can be counted. With more, "Fewer dots can." appears and Fewest stays greyed for that many dots or more. Undo takes back moves but keeps that answer (the `carry` hook), so pressing Fewest and undoing can't be used to ask whether a count is the fewest.
- **Why the app draws the Fewest argument.** "No fewer dots can do it" is a statement about every placement of fewer dots; nothing on the board can be tapped to certify it. The child supplies the tappable half, a witness with that many dots; the argument (a closed region needs three sides, each with its own dot, and parallel sides need two more) is drawn by the app after a right claim and explained in the grown-up notes.
- **Copy.** Each puzzle shows one short objective above the board: "Make A's region exactly the shaded part." (1, 3–5, 10), "Make A's region and B's region stop touching." (2), "Make one place belong to four dots." (6), "Make D's region touch A's and B's, but not C's." (7), "Take S from A, but keep P and Q with A." (8; R in 11, P alone in 12) and "Fit A's whole region in the frame, with the fewest dots." (9). How to play gives the rules: every place belongs to the nearest dot and ties belong to all; a region is all its places and the map goes on past the frame; new dots go where grid lines cross inside the frame and regions stay hidden until Check; plus one line for each kind (exactly the shaded part everywhere; touching includes a single point; rings mark shared places; "If it can't be done, press Can't after a Check and tap two places that show why.", which doesn't say which two; Fewest).
- **Hints.** Before any move, the first hint is the authored nudge, which never names the answer and holds wherever the child's dots go (puzzle 6's "Without D, A, B and C share one place. Put D in a corner and press Check to find it." is checked with D in each corner). After a move, the hint card gives the idea for the board as it stands and never the spot: the puzzle's idea ("The edge between two dots is halfway between them."), why a dot has to go ("A dot nearer to P than A is takes P from A.", "A's region still fits in the frame with one dot fewer."), or "Press Check." and "Press Fewest." when that is the step. The second level rings the spot on the board (the dot and where it goes, or the button), and the screen reader hears it in words measured from a named dot ("Move the dot to the spot 2 squares right of A."); the third offers Apply hint. So one move and one Hint no longer hand over the answer. In puzzle 11 the hints lead to a try first ("Try to take R from A with a dot, and press Check.", "Press Check.") and then give the reason with Can't: "R is on the straight line from P to Q, and A's region never has a dent. Press Can't."; "Press Can't." is never the first hint, and the ring marks P and Q. In the fit puzzle they keep as many of the child's dots as they can. Hints alone finish every puzzle from a fresh start, after a wrong dot, after random play, after a refused Can't and after a refused Fewest, and the validator checks that no hint's words name a spot. Each hint is remembered per board, and the slowest (the first in puzzle 6, cold) takes about 15–50 ms on a laptop.
- **Phone size.** At 560 px wide or less the board reaches the edges of the play panel and its margin past the frame shrinks to a quarter square. Measured in the phone smoke (375 × 812, `TEST_PHONE=1`): the board is 339 px wide and crossings are 26.1 px apart (about 4.4 mm on a phone), up from 22.4 px on a 313 px board; whole squares are 52 px. On a 1024 px desktop the board is 440 px and crossings are 31.4 px apart.
- **Focus.** After a move, focus lands on the board (or, after a solve, on the completion heading); Check, Fewest and the Can't picks go grey or away after use, so focus does not stay on them. It never lands on Can't.

## The playground

Two dots to start, at (−1.5, −1) and (1.5, 1) as in K–1 Problem 2, and up to ten, each with a letter and a colour. Regions are always shown and are redrawn while a dot is dragged. There is no goal, no Check and no Hint, and it never counts as solved. Its notes suggest a third dot between two, four at the corners of a square, and the question whether adding a dot can ever give an old dot more places (grades 4–5 Problem 5: no).

## Rules that keep the record honest

- The save is the new dots, sorted, and whether Check has been pressed since the last change; in the take puzzles also the right claim or the refused pair; in the fit puzzles also the done flag and the smallest count at which Fewest was refused; in the playground, the dots in the order they were put down. Everything shown is derived from it.
- Only legal moves are accepted: a dot on a free grid point inside the frame within the budget (not on a fixed dot or another new dot), a move to a free grid point, taking away a dot that is there, Check once per change, Can't only in the take puzzles, after a checked try, with two different places and not the pair just refused, Fewest only in the fit puzzles after a Check that shows the region in the frame and below a refused count, and nothing after a solve. In the playground, at most ten dots and no Check.
- Saved boards are checked: whole-number points in half-squares inside the frame, no doubles, none on a fixed dot, sorted, within the budget; a claim only of the certifying pair and only with a checked try, a refusal only of a pair that does not certify and only with a checked try, never both; done only with the fewest dots checked and fitting; a refused count above the fewest and within the budget; no unknown fields.
- **Exact arithmetic.** Points are stored in half-squares, so every dot and place has whole-number coordinates. A half-plane is a·u + b·v ≤ c with whole numbers, and a corner is a triple of whole numbers (X, Y, D) meaning (X/D, Y/D); with dots within 6 half-squares of the middle, every number stays far below 2⁵³, so every edge, tie, touch and fit is exact (`dist/families/sites/plane.js`).
- The answers are computed by `scripts/build-sites.mjs` with the mechanic's geometry (corners among every pair of lines) and checked again by `scripts/validate-sites.mjs` by other methods.

## Not yet done

- **Children's play.** Nothing here has been tried by children. Three things to watch: whether hiding the regions until Check leads children to predict an edge before pressing it, or to press Check after every dot (if Check becomes a reflex, a short "Where will the edge be?" moment, or limiting free Checks, may help); whether children see a mirror image (as far past the edge as A is before it) or keep nudging a dot toward the shaded edge itself, as puzzle 1's notes expect; and whether Can't in puzzle 11 is found by reasoning about the segment, or by trying pairs until one is accepted (there are only three pairs, so the try and the drawn segment carry the argument; watch whether the segment is understood).
- **Tap size on a phone.** At 375 px wide, crossings are 26.1 px apart (half a square). Taps land on the nearest crossing and a dragged dot rides above the finger, but nobody has yet watched a five-year-old place dots on a phone; a zoomed board is the next step if they can't.
- **Dropped in review.** "Close B in" (B between A and C on a line, the fewest dots to close B's region in, 2; grades 4–5 Problem 6) was dropped so that puzzle 2 could join at Easy with twelve puzzles balanced four, four and four; the reviewer found it played easier than Close A in. It could return in an encore set.
- **Fewest without a certificate to tap.** The "can't with fewer" side is drawn by the app after a right Fewest, not supplied by the child (see How a puzzle plays). A tappable version (tap the sides of the region and count them) is possible but not built.
- **Triangles as certificates.** Can't takes a pair of places; a place inside the triangle A, P, Q (off its sides) would need all three, and no puzzle asks for it.
- **Story.** Not on the Lantern Road; no keeper lines or art.
- **Grade levels.** Grade-free; not yet in a K–1, 2–3 or 4–5 trail. The worksheet's drawing tasks (draw the regions of given dots, explain why a boundary works for every point) and its "which dot is nearest to each cross" warm-ups stay on paper, as the review card decided.
