# Flat folds

October 17, 2026. Twelve puzzles and a playground built from Week 28 of the Bellingham math circle (single-vertex flat folding). They are in the puzzle satchel only, not on the Lantern Road. Nothing here has been played by children, and no paper model of the worksheet has been folded. The theme's [review card](https://github.com/jamesrp/math-circle-worksheets/blob/main/plans/review/week-28.md) says revise (for the worksheet's missing kit, which doesn't hold up the port), and its Decision is what this family builds: the child taps rays to cycle mountain and valley and presses Fold; the app searches the layer orders and animates the collapse or shows the collision; the solves are completing labels round printed ones, sharing a stock of M and V tabs across disks, and dragging in the ray that lets a disk close; the "can't fold" certificates are the alternating fans, the clashing colours and the tab count, which the child supplies after Can't. There are no "will it fold?" quizzes: every puzzle asks the child to make a fold or to show why none exists. Each puzzle's `provenance` names its worksheet source.

## The mathematics

A paper disk is creased along rays from its centre, ray 1 at the top and the rest clockwise; the **wedges** are the pieces between neighbouring rays, and the wedge after ray k runs clockwise from ray k to the next ray. Every ray is folded all the way flat, as a **mountain** (M, a ridge rising towards you from the yellow front) or a **valley** (V, sinking away). Layers may touch but not pass through one another.

- **Every crease turns the paper over.** Flattened, neighbouring wedges face opposite ways, so going round the disk the wedges face up, down, up, … and the rays land at 0, a1, a1 − a2, a1 − a2 + a3, … degrees. This is the picture the app folds (the side view below the disk is the rim of the disk as a strip, each wedge a layer, yellow or green by the side facing up).
- **An odd number of rays never folds** (puzzle 4, seven rays): the last wedge meets the first across ray 1 facing the same way, though ray 1 is a crease. A colouring of the wedges by the side facing up must alternate across every ray, and an odd ring of wedges can't be coloured that way.
- **Kawasaki's condition** (puzzles 1, 7, 8, 10 and 12): with an even number of rays the strip closes only when the alternating sums are equal, each a half-turn, 180°. With the letters free it is also enough (Kawasaki–Justin), so for the ray puzzles exactly one tick lets the disk close (validator: every tick tried).
- **Maekawa's theorem**: in any flat fold the mountains and valleys differ by two, so four rays fold with three of one letter and one of the other (puzzles 2 and 5), eight rays with five and three (puzzle 9).
- **Big-little-big**: the two creases round a wedge smaller than both its neighbours bend opposite ways (puzzle 5: the 30° wedge of 30°, 60°, 150°, 120° rules out VVVM, which passes the count). Round a run of equal smallest wedges between bigger ones, the creases split as evenly as they can: an odd run of wedges has as many mountains as valleys among its creases, an even run one more of either (puzzle 2: rays 4, 1 and 2 round the two 60° wedges can't all match; puzzle 9: rays 1 to 4 round three 30° wedges take two of each, and with the count and big-little-big that leaves 2 of 32 labellings).
- **The crimp reduction** (Bern–Hayes, Hull) decides every labelling: crimp away a wedge no larger than its neighbours whose two creases differ, and check the smaller vertex; a labelling folds exactly when this ends in a straight fold.
- **Right angles and parity** (puzzles 3, 6 and 11): four right angles fold in exactly the 8 labellings with an odd number of mountains, so a disk with printed letters takes M tabs of one parity (none or two, or exactly one), and a stock of tabs can be impossible by counting alone: K–1 row B's three disks take 2 or 4 M tabs, never 3.

What the app computes: a fold is a choice of **layer order** (which wedge lies on which) such that every crease bends the way its letter says, no crease passes through a layer that covers its place, and two creases at one place neither interleave (same side) nor wrap through each other (opposite sides). `dist/families/creases/fold.js` searches the orders from the bottom up with branch and bound, memoised per disk and letters; eight rays take well under 100 ms (tests time it in CPU time). A labelling that doesn't fold is drawn with the attempt of fewest problems, its wrongly bent creases and the crossings marked in rust. The validator recomputes every answer by the crimp reduction (written afresh), by a brute force over every layer order with `foldcheck.py`'s three conditions, and against the answers `foldcheck.py` printed, and checks the mechanic against the crimp on 76 random disks of 4, 6 and 8 rays.

Experiments come first: label, Fold, and watch the strip fold flat or jam. The conjectures (the count of letters, the small wedge, the half-turns, odd never) come from folding several disks; the explanations are in each puzzle's grown-up notes, and in the hints only after an attempt.

"Random taps that win" is the validator's count of how often random play solves the puzzle (random letters on the open rays; a random use of all the tabs; a random tick and letters for the new ray) or, for a can't, how often a random certificate is accepted (a random finished colouring; a random marking of every disk). The validator checks each count exactly, and against a ceiling for its kind (a fold's letters at most 1 in 4 at Easy and Medium, where two open rays can't do better, and 1 in 10 at Hard; a colouring or a marking at most 1 in 8; tab placements at most 0.3, 0.2 and 0.15 by level; a ray 1 in 20). Printed letters appear on folds that work (2, 5, 9) and on folds that can't (4, 10), so letters on the board say nothing about the answer. Right-angle disks fold with half of all labellings, so they appear only with printed letters and a shared stock (the card's pitfall); the right-angle playground disk has no goal.

## How a puzzle plays

- **The disk.** Wedges are yellow (the front) with their angles written in them; rays are solid for a mountain, dashed for a valley and dotted while open, numbered just inside the rim. Each ray has a letter chip outside the rim: dark for a printed letter, paper for the child's, dashed while open. Ticks every 15° mark the rim.
- **Labels.** Tap anywhere inside the disk near a ray (within 20°, so in a 30° wedge every tap goes to the nearer ray), or its chip, to switch it: open, M, V, M, … A printed letter doesn't change. Enter or Space on a focused chip does the same.
- **What a tap reaches.** Each chip that takes taps carries an invisible shape matching the disk's own taps for its ray (as far round as the nearer of 20° or halfway to the next ray in a fold, 45° on a tab disk; in a ray puzzle, 7.5° in the rim band) and running on past the chip to the edge of the picture, so a tap just outside the rim beside a chip letters its ray. In the playground a chip reaches 7.5° either side of its ray inside the disk, where the disk's taps letter it too, and press-and-hold is watched on the whole disk, chips included. On a 375 px phone every such chip is 40 px or more across, the new ray's knob 41 px, the chips' letters at least 13.8 px and the ray numbers 13.3 px. The tab disks were also losing 20 % of their size to a shared `.small` button rule; their class is now `cr-small`.
- **Fold.** With every ray labelled, **Fold** folds the rim up as a strip below the disk, animated once from flat to folded (skipped with reduced motion). A flat fold solves the puzzle. A jam keeps the attempt of fewest problems: its rays and chips in rust, a rust bend for a crease that turns the wrong way, a cross where a crease would pass through a layer; on a disk that can't close, the two edges of ray 1 that should meet are joined by a dashed rust line. Fold waits for a change of letters. The ray puzzles have no Can't: a ray always works.
- **Tabs** (puzzles 3, 6 and 11). Small right-angle disks A, B, C (and D) with printed letters, and a row of M and V tabs above them. Tap a disk near an open ray (any tap goes to the nearest ray) or its chip to put a tab on: M, then V, then off, skipping a letter whose tabs are all used. Fold needs every tab on; it marks each disk that folds with a green rim and each that jams in rust (with the side view under each disk on wider screens).
- **The new ray** (puzzles 1, 7 and 12). A round knob waits beside the disk. Drag it round the rim, tap it (each tap steps it to the next free tick clockwise), tap near the rim, or use the arrow keys on it, to put the new ray on a free tick; the wedges show their new angles. Before the new ray is placed, a tap inside the disk away from the rays places it too; once it is placed, a tap inside the disk letters the nearest ray (within 20°, as in a fold) and never moves the new ray, which moves only by the knob, the keys or a tap in the rim band (the outer 14 units of the disk, where a tap within 7.5° of a ray's end letters that ray). Label every ray and press Fold; a ray in the wrong place shows the strip's ends apart.
- **Copy.** One short objective, because the board can't say what is asked: "Fold the disk flat.", "Use every tab so all the disks fold." or "Add a ray so the disk folds flat.". How to play opens with the mathematics ("A crease is a mountain (M) when it rises towards you from the yellow front and a valley (V) when it sinks away; the wedges are the pieces between the rays.") and then the gestures.
- **Hints.** Before any move, the first hint is the authored nudge, which always asks for an attempt. Then: the next letter toward the nearest labelling that folds ("Make ray 4 a valley."), the tab to take off or put on ("Put a V tab on the left ray of disk A."), where the new ray goes ("Put the new ray between ray 3 and ray 1, 8 ticks after ray 3."), and "Press Fold.". For a can't: open rays labelled M, Fold, then the reason with Can't ("Every crease turns the paper over, so every other wedge faces up. The wedges facing up make 135° and the others 225°, but folded flat each set must make a half-turn, 180°."), then the colouring a wedge at a time ("Colour the wedge from ray 1 to ray 2 yellow.") or each disk's mark ("Tap disk A until it shows two tabs the same."), then Check. The second level marks the chip, wedge, disk, knob place or button; the third offers Apply hint. Hints alone finish every puzzle from a fresh start, after a wrong move, after a refused claim and after Undo.

## Certificates and claims

- **Can't comes after an attempt.** Can't stays disabled until a Fold has failed, so the certificate is made round the child's own attempt and a fresh board solves nothing. Pressing Can't again puts the colours (or the marks) away.
- **The colouring (puzzles 4, 8 and 10).** After Can't the child colours every wedge yellow or green by the side it would show facing up (tap: yellow, then green, …) and presses **Check**, judged once with every wedge coloured. On an odd disk it is accepted when exactly one pair of neighbours match: the clash is ringed at its ray ("The wedges on both sides of ray 1 face the same way."). On an even disk it is accepted when the colours alternate (and the alternate wedges don't make half-turns, which the builder checks for every can't), and the app then draws the two fans, the yellow wedges and the green wedges each laid from a straight edge against a half-turn, with their sums (135° and 225°) and the shortfall and overshoot in rust. A wrong colouring gets one refusal, the same words on every disk ("Not that colouring."), which don't say what the rule is, and is cleared; a refusal costs nothing else. Nothing marks the wedges to tap: an even disk with n wedges is certified by 2 of 2ⁿ colourings, and an odd disk with n wedges by 2n of 2ⁿ, since any colouring with exactly one clash is a fair argument. No colouring rule on five wedges does better while still accepting every correct argument, so the odd disk has seven wedges: 14 of 128, about 11%.
- **Why the child colours rather than taps a sum.** The fans are the card's certificate, but which wedges make a fan is exactly the argument: the colouring is the child pointing at the fans (alternating) or at the forced clash (odd). The sums are then drawn by the app, because they are arithmetic, not a choice.
- **The tab count (puzzle 6).** After Can't the child marks each disk with the tabs it can take, the split the adult guide's proof uses (guide p. 8: the two choices for disk A beside the two forced one-M disks). The whole disk is the button; under it two empty tab boxes wait, the first tap shows M V (one M tab and one V tab), the next V V or M M (two the same), and so on. Check waits for every disk and accepts exactly the true marks (A: two the same; B and C: one of each), whichever disk the child reasoned from; then "The M tabs used are 2 or 4, never 3." appears. Any other marking gets "Not those tabs." and is cleared; 1 of the 8 markings is accepted.
- **Refusals stay.** Can't on a puzzle that can be done gets "There is a way." after Check, with no values. It is saved, and Can't and Check stay off, through Undo, until a letter or a tab changes; Undo takes back the colouring and the fold one step at a time, and each step changes the board.
- **No answers on the board.** Titles, objectives, How to play and first hints never name the answer, the parity or the half-turn rule; those appear in hints after an attempt and in the grown-up notes.

## The playground

Four right-angle rays to start. Tap inside the disk away from the rays to add a ray on the nearest tick (up to eight), tap a ray (anywhere along it) or its chip to switch its letter, and press and hold a ray for about half a second (or press Delete on its chip) to take it away; moving or lifting first cancels, so a quick tap never removes a ray. The knob beside the disk adds a ray from the keyboard (arrow keys choose a tick, Enter adds it) or by dragging it in. Fold folds any disk, odd or even, and shows the flat fold or the jam; Clear takes every ray away. There is no goal and no Hint, and it never counts as solved.

## Rules that keep the record honest

- The save is the letters, the last failed labelling (`tried`), the colouring while Can't is on, the refusal (`told`) and done (fold); each disk's letters, the last failed tabs, each disk's mark while Can't is on, the refusal and done (tabs); the new ray's tick, the letters (new ray last), the last failed try and done (ray); the rays, letters and last folded disk (playground). Everything drawn is derived from it.
- Only legal moves are accepted: a known ray or disk, M or V (or off, for a tab), never on a printed letter; no more tabs of a letter than the stock; the new ray on a free tick; Fold only with every ray labelled and a change since the last try; Can't only after a failed Fold and not after There is a way; colours and marks only while Can't is on; Check only with every wedge coloured or every disk marked, and not after There is a way; nothing after a solve.
- Saved boards are checked: letters that keep the printed ones; a tried labelling that really fails; colours only after a fold; There is a way only on a puzzle that can be done, and a refused colouring or marking (cleared) only on one that can't; done only on letters that fold, on a certifying colouring of an impossible disk, on exactly the true marks, or on the one tick with letters that fold; the playground's rays on ticks, in order, at most eight.
- The answers are computed by `scripts/build-creases.mjs` with the mechanic's search and checked against the table above, and again by `scripts/validate-creases.mjs` with the crimp reduction, a brute force over layer orders and `foldcheck.py`'s printed answers.

## Not yet done

- **Children's play.** Nothing here has been tried by children, and the worksheet itself is unpiloted with no paper model folded. Four things to watch: whether children read the side view (a strip seen edge-on) as the disk folded up, or need the paper disk beside the screen first; whether, after Can't, children colour by "the side facing up" or colour at random until Check accepts (the seven-wedge disk accepts 14 of 128, and refusals are free); whether children find press-and-hold to remove a ray in the playground, which only How to play mentions; and whether the tab disks and their mark boxes are big enough to tap and read on a phone, where four disks share the screen.
- **The odd certificate.** Any colouring with exactly one clash is accepted, because each is a fair proof, so chance is about 11% on seven wedges. A refused colouring costs no fold; if children colour at random, the next step would be a different certificate, not a penalty.
- **Side view on phones.** Tab puzzles on screens narrower than 420 px show folds and jams on the disks only (green rim, rust rays), without the strip, so the buttons stay on screen.
