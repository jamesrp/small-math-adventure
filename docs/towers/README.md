# Tower cities

October 10, 2026. Twelve puzzles built from Week 5 of the Bellingham math circle (tower cities: how many towers you see from an end). They are a group, **Towers**, inside Symbol Orchard, and are in the puzzle satchel only, not on the Lantern Road. Nothing here has been played by children, and the Week 5 worksheet (v4) is unpiloted. The theme's [review card](https://github.com/jamesrp/math-circle-worksheets/blob/main/plans/review/week-05.md) decides what is built: James chose on October 10 to port it as a group of its own in Symbol Orchard that reuses the Orchard's board, so the Orchard's own puzzles keep their one rule (one of each symbol in every row and column) and their saves. The solves are the Decision's: rows for a card or a checked "No row", one-city puzzles and a checked "No city", every city then one more number ending in That's all, making a puzzle while the app plays the partner and shows a second city, and twin cities. No puzzle asks "how many will you see?", and the counting problems stay on paper.

## The mathematics

A **row** of n towers, of heights 1 to n once each, is a permutation. Looking along it from one end you see a tower when it is taller than every tower in front of it: the towers seen are the **records** (left-to-right maxima) of the permutation read from that end. An end number, or card, gives L seen from the left and R from the right.

- **The tallest is seen from both ends, and it hides everything behind it.** So the towers seen from the left stand at or before the tallest and those from the right at or after it. With the tallest in place j (from 1), **L ≤ j and R ≤ n + 1 − j**, so L + R ≤ n + 1.
- **The per-end rule.** L = 1 exactly when the tallest stands first from the left. For L ≥ 2, the tallest can stand in place j with L seen exactly when j ≥ L (the j − 1 towers in front of it can show any number of records from 1 to j − 1). The same holds from the right.
- **Which cards have a row.** For n ≥ 2, a row shows (L, R) exactly when L + R ≤ n + 1 and the card is not (1, 1): the per-end rules leave a common place for the tallest. Otherwise the two ends' places for the tallest do not meet, and that is the certificate for "No row" (puzzles 2 and 4). Equality L + R = n + 1 holds for the 2^(n−1) rows that rise to the tallest and then fall; the number of rows with (L, R) is C(L + R − 2, L − 1) times the Stirling number c(n − 1, L + R − 2) (Stanley, EC1, §1.3).

A **city** is an n-by-n Latin square (one tower of each height in every row and column); there are 12 of order 3 and 576 of order 4 (OEIS A002860). Its **edge numbers** count the towers seen along each row and column from each side, 4n of them.

- **A set of edge numbers can fit one city, several or none.** Each number allows its line only the orders with that many records; a city fits when every line is allowed. Placing the tallest is the usual first step: a 1 puts it next to the number, and a k ≥ 2 keeps it at least k − 1 squares in.
- **"No city" has a pointable certificate here.** If every square of some line is barred to that line's tallest tower by one edge number through the square (the per-end rule, along the line or across it), no city fits. Puzzle 6 is the worksheet's: 2 above each of three columns leaves row 1's 3 no square, since a 3 at the top of a column would make that column show 1. This test is sound for every set of numbers (no city's own numbers ever bar a line), but it is not complete: a 1 left of row 2 and a 1 left of row 3 fit no 3-by-3 city (both 3s would be in column 1), yet no single line is barred. Puzzle 6 has one, and the app accepts exactly the lines barred this way.
- **Fewest numbers.** On 3-by-3 one number never fixes a city (swap the two lines parallel to its own that it does not look along), and every city is fixed by 13 of its 66 pairs. On 4-by-4 no two numbers fix any city, and only 208 of the 576 cities are fixed by three; these are computer facts (checked in the validator), not something children are asked to prove.
- **Twins.** The twelve 3-by-3 cities show twelve different sets of numbers, but 204 of the 576 4-by-4 cities share all sixteen with another city: 66 classes (34 of two, 28 of four, 4 of six), 262 pairs, 152 of which differ by switching a 2-by-2 block. In puzzle 12 the middle block 1 4 / 4 1 sits between 2s and 3s at both ends of its rows and columns, so the 1s are hidden and the 4s seen either way.

Experiments come first: swap towers and look from an end, build cities and look along a full line, show numbers and watch the second city. The conjectures (seeing 1 means the tallest is at that end; L + R can't be too big; one number never fixes a 3-by-3 city) come from those attempts. The explanations (the tallest hides everything behind it; a line with no place for its tallest) are drawn on the child's own board after a right claim and spelled out in the parent notes. Counting rows by records (4–5 Problems 7 and 8, 2–3 Problem 7) stays on paper.

## How a puzzle plays

- **Rows.** The towers stand between two end numbers in circles. Tap a tower, then another, to swap them, or drag one onto another. Tap an end number to look from that end: an eye appears, the towers it sees turn sun-yellow and the hidden ones grey (tap again to stop looking). The row is checked as it stands: when both ends see their numbers it is solved.
- **No row.** The button is off until the child has made a swap, so the claim is always about an attempt. A wrong No row is refused once and for good ("A row fits.", button off; Undo takes back swaps, not the refusal). A right one draws the reason under the child's own row: two lanes of ghost towers, one for each end number, showing where that number lets the tallest stand, a red ✕ in a lane under the child's tallest where that end number bars it, and the child's tallest outlined. The lanes never meet.
- **Why the app draws the row certificate.** The certificate is a statement about every place in the row (for each place, one end number bars the tallest), and a row has only one line, so there is nothing for a child to choose and tap: pointing at their own tallest would refute one place, not all. So the app draws the whole argument after a right claim, round the child's attempt. In cities, where the choice of line is the argument, the child taps it.
- **Cities.** The Orchard's grid with an edge place on every side. Tap a square, then a height on the keypad below (or type the digit; Clear or Backspace empties it). Each square fills with a leaf-green bar as tall as its height. A height repeated in a row or column is marked as in the Orchard. When a row or column is full, its edge numbers become buttons: tap one to look along that line (towers seen in sun, hidden ones grey, and a side view of the line's towers below the grid, read in the grid's direction with the eye on the side looked from). Sight lines are never offered on unfinished lines. When the grid is a full city that misses some edge numbers, those numbers get an ochre ring.
- **No city.** Off until the child has placed a height. Press it, then tap beside the row or above the column whose tallest tower has no place (every edge place becomes a dashed target). If a city fits, the claim is refused for good ("A city fits."). If none fits but no edge number bars the tapped line's tallest from one of its squares, that square is ringed ("No edge number rules this square out.") and the child may tap another line. The right line solves the puzzle: its squares are crossed and the edge numbers that bar them are ringed in red, on the child's own grid.
- **Every city** (7 and 10). Each city that fits is kept the moment it is built, as a small grid below; there are no slots and no count. **That's all** says "There is another." and stays off until a new city is found (Undo keeps that, and the cities found). When every city is found, That's all opens the second part: the grid shows a found city (tap one below to show it), the empty edge places become dashed buttons, and the child taps one and a number (or types it) to add it. Cities the added number rules out dim; the puzzle is solved when only one fits.
- **Make a puzzle** (8 and 11). The city is shown with its edge places empty. Tap a place to show the city's number there, again to hide it; beads show the budget. Beside the city the partner shows another city that fits the numbers shown, with the squares where it differs marked, as soon as there is one; when none is left, the puzzle is solved. The budget is the card's (2 for 3-by-3, 3 for 4-by-4): it is a limit to make a puzzle within, not a count to beat, and the puzzle never asks why it is the fewest.
- **Twins** (12). The first city is in a small grid beside the board; the board starts as a copy of it. Change heights until it is a different city with the same sixteen numbers.
- **Copy.** One short line under the title per kind: "Make each end see its number.", "Build a city that fits the edge numbers.", "Find every city that fits, then add one number so only one does.", "Show numbers so that only this city fits.", "Build a different city with the same sixteen numbers." How to play gives the rules ("You see a tower when it is taller than every tower in front of it.") and the controls.
- **Hints.** Before any move the first hint is the authored nudge; titles and first hints never name the answer or say that a puzzle is impossible. Then hints are computed from the board: rows swap toward the nearest row that works ("Swap the 1 and the 4."); cities change a wrong height first and then fill the square settled earliest by going line by line ("Put 3 in row 1, column 1. Only 3 fits there.", or "Try 1 in …" for a guess); every-city puzzles aim at the nearest city not yet found, then "Press That's all.", then "Write 3 above column 2."; make-a-puzzle hides a number outside the best set or shows the next one. For an impossible goal the chain first makes an attempt ("Try a row: swap the 2 and the 3.", "Try a 3 in row 1, column 1."), then gives the reason before the claim ("Seeing 1 from the left puts the 3 at place 1. Seeing 1 from the right puts it at place 3. Press No row."; "Wherever the 3 of row 1 stands, the 2 above it would see only that 3. Press No city, then tap left of row 1."). "Press No row." is never a first hint. The second level marks the squares, towers or places on the board and the third offers Apply hint. Hints alone finish every puzzle from a fresh start, after a wrong swap or height, after a wrong claim, after a wrong That's all and after an added number that leaves several cities. Hints and searches are memoised per board; the slowest hint in the validator takes under 20 ms.
- **Keyboard.** Every tower, square, edge place and key is a button; digits type into the chosen square (the Orchard's handler) or, after That's all, into the chosen edge place. After a move focus returns to the board or its keys (the module's focus list holds towers, squares and edge places), never to No row, No city or That's all.

## Rules that keep the record honest

- The save is the row, a No-row answer, a refusal and whether a swap has been made; or the grid's heights, the No-city line, a missed line, a refusal and whether a height has been placed; or the heights, the cities found in order, "There is another.", That's all and the added number; or the edge places shown; or the twin grid. Everything shown (sight lines, rings, crosses, the second city) is derived from it and the puzzle.
- Only legal moves are accepted: swaps of two different places; heights 0 to n in a square that changes; No row and No city only after an attempt and not after a refusal; No city with an edge place; That's all only while it is open; added numbers only after That's all, on an empty edge place; shown places only within the budget; nothing after a solve.
- Saved boards are checked: rows and grids of the right size and heights; a No row or No city kept only when none fits and only after an attempt, and a refusal only when one fits; a No-city line only when it is barred, and a missed line only when it is not; every found city fits, once; a city that fits on the grid is in the found list; "There is another." only while one is missing; That's all only with every city found; an added number only after That's all, on an empty place; shown places in order within the budget.
- Undo restores moves, not claims: a refusal, the cities found and "There is another." are carried across it. Taking back a refused claim or a wrong That's all then changes nothing on the screen, so the same press also takes back the move before it.
- The answers are computed by `scripts/build-towers.mjs` with the mechanic's own searches and checked against this table, then checked again by `scripts/validate-towers.mjs` by other methods.
