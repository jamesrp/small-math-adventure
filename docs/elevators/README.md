# Doubling elevators

October 10, 2026. Twelve puzzles and a playground built from Week 74 of the Bellingham math circle (Doubling elevators). They are in the puzzle satchel only, not on the Lantern Road. No child has played them, and the Week 74 packet is an unpiloted prototype too (one shared Grades 4–5 packet; marker and partner handling unrehearsed). The theme's [review card](https://github.com/jamesrp/math-circle-worksheets/blob/main/plans/review/week-74.md) says keep, rates the fit A and size S, and asks for what the app can do and paper can't: enforce the stride. Its App fit shaped the puzzles: reach a flag within a par proved optimal, the reverse task (farthest within a budget), and the step-back saving at 23, 31, 47 and 63; no "2 of 2" counter on tied trips, no predicting a landing, and no claim that the board is the whole Baumslag–Solitar group.

## The mathematics

A **car** stands on a whole-number ground coordinate x at a level h ≥ 0. A **move** rides up a level, rides down a level (never below the ground), or steps left or right, and a step on level h is 2^h long: 1 on the ground, then 2, 4, 8, 16, 32. Every trip starts at 0 on the ground and ends on the ground, and every move costs one. A trip is a word in U, D, L and R; `UURRDD` reaches 8.

- **The bound.** A trip of at most N moves that climbs to level H spends at least 2H moves riding (H up and H down), and each of its other moves goes at most 2^H, so it ends at most (N − 2H)·2^H from 0. This covers left steps, repeated climbs and steps on lower levels: they only add moves or shorten steps (the triangle inequality). Climbing straight to H, stepping right N − 2H times and riding down reaches the bound, so **the farthest a trip of N moves can end is the largest of (N − 2H)·2^H over H**: 4, 6, 8, 12, 16 for budgets 4 to 8 (Week 74 Problem 3), 24 for 9, 32 for 10 and 48 for 11. So 16 needs 8 moves, by exactly two trips (UUURRDDD and UURRRRDD; Problems 2 and 4), and seven moves never reach it: (7 − 2H)·2^H is 7, 10, 12 or 8.
- **Distance shrinks exponentially.** Coordinate 2^k is only about 2k moves away. In the Baumslag–Solitar group BS(1, 2) = ⟨a, t | t⁻¹at = a²⟩, t⁻ᵏ a tᵏ = a^(2^k), so a^(2^k) is a word of 2k + 1 letters: the cyclic subgroup generated by a is exponentially distorted (Druţu and Kapovich, *Geometric Group Theory*, §8.9, Definition 8.94). The board is the part of the Cayley graph of BS(1, 2) on the elements a^x t^(−h) with h ≥ 0, where R is a, L is a⁻¹ and U is t⁻¹: a toy model, as the worksheet says, not the whole group. Leaving out the rest of the group can only make board distances longer; the puzzles use only the board.
- **Going too far can be shorter.** 23 takes 10 moves by going to 24 (UUURRRDDD) and stepping back one, while every trip with no left step needs 11. Without left steps every step adds, like coins worth 1, 2, 4, … up to the top level's step; topping out at H, the fewest steps to n are ⌊n / 2^H⌋ plus the number of ones in n mod 2^H in binary, so the fewest moves are the least of 2H plus that (for 23: 23, 14, 11, 11, 12 for H = 0 to 4). The same happens at 31 (11 against 12), 47 (12 against 14) and 63 (13 against 15), and up to 64 exactly at 23, 31, 39, 46, 47, 55, 62 and 63. The fewest moves relate to writing the number in binary with digits allowed to be −1: 23 = 16 + 8 − 1 has three nonzero digits where 10111 has four.

`scripts/validate-elevators.mjs` checks these statements over every case it can list: every word of up to eleven moves on an open board (1.8 million), for the bound and for which trips reach it; the step-back numbers up to 64 by a separate search; and the coin count for every n up to 64.

The worksheets are in [math-circle-worksheets, week 74](https://github.com/jamesrp/math-circle-worksheets/tree/main/lowell-math-circle-year-2/week-74): a pawn or marker on a printed board of levels 0–4 and x from −4 to 24, with a partner checking each move and writing the word. Puzzles 1, 5 and 9 use the worksheet's targets; puzzles 2, 3, 4, 8 and 12 turn Problem 3's budgets into flags or ask Problem 3 with budgets 9 and 11; 6, 7, 10 and 11 are new.

Budgets are the fewest moves on an unbounded board. `scripts/build-elevators.mjs` computes them by breadth-first search on a board from −400 to 600 with ten levels, checks that each puzzle's window keeps every shortest trip (the counts above are the same in the window as unbounded), that each window has one spare level above the highest shortest trip (puzzle 11 has none: six lines is the most), and, for the farthest puzzles, that the farthest ground point within the budget equals the bound and is the same inside the window. The validator recomputes all of it by iterative deepening over move words.

## How a puzzle plays

- **The board.** The levels are horizontal lines, the ground at the bottom, with a dot at every whole-number coordinate in the window. Each level's step length (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32) sits at its left, in Georgia numerals; ground coordinates are marked every 4, or every 8 on wide boards. The car is a small pine elevator car; the flag stands on the ground at the target. The trail stays on the board: a riser for each ride and a shallow arc over the dots for each step, so an arc on level 3 spans eight dots. Columns are placed in percent and levels in pixels, so the board fills the width of a phone or a tablet while the car and the labels keep their size.
- **Moves.** Four buttons under the board, an inverted T of arrows, named "Up", "Down", "Left 4", "Right 4" with the step at the car's level. Arrow keys work on the board and its buttons. The places one move away carry a ring, and a tap on the board goes to the ring nearest the tap (the car counts as a candidate, so a tap on the car does nothing). Down is disabled on the ground, Up on the top level, and a step that would leave the window is disabled. The car slides to its new place, along the arc for a step (not with reduced motion).
- **Budget.** An outline under the board stands for each move; each move fills one with its arrow. The budget is always the fewest moves, so a wasted move means Undo, and the board then says so ("Too few moves are left. Undo.", or "Some trip goes farther. Undo." on the farthest puzzles).
- **Solved** when the car is on the ground at the target within the budget. Flag puzzles show no objective line (`visibleObjective: ''`). The two farthest puzzles have no flag, so they keep one line, "Go as far right as you can."; How to play says the trip must end on the ground, as far right as any trip with these moves can end. A trip that ends on the ground short of that is not solved.
- **Hints.** Before any move, the first hint is the authored nudge ("Count the steps.", "Try going past the flag."). Then hints name the next move of a shortest finish within the moves left, searched from where the car is ("Go up.", "Step right 8."), with the button and the ring marked in ochre, or say Undo when no finish fits. The third level offers Apply hint, and hints alone finish every puzzle, from a fresh board, after a wasted move and with the budget spent.

## The playground

Levels 0–5 and x from −8 to 72, every move, the last 24 moves shown as arrows, and **Clear**. There is no flag, no Hint, and it never counts as solved.

## Rules that keep the record honest

- Only legal moves are accepted: never below the ground, above the top level or off the window; no move past the budget or after a solve. The playground takes moves inside its window, up to 200.
- The save is the move word, and everything shown is derived from it. A save is rejected if it has any other letter, replays off the board, runs past the budget, or reaches the target before its last move.

## Not yet done

- **Children's play.** Nothing here has been tried by children. Things to watch: whether children plan the climb before moving (counting two moves a level against longer steps), or press Up until the steps look big and then walk; whether the step numbers at the left of the levels are read as step lengths, and whether children see the arc on a high level cover many dots; and whether, on the dense boards (puzzles 7, 10, 11 and 12 on a phone, about 4 to 6 pixels a dot), the last step of 1 onto the flag is visible enough, and the farthest puzzles' "Go as far right as you can" reads as a goal without a flag.
- **Where the review card differs.** The card proposes a board that scrolls past the window; here each puzzle has a fixed window that holds every shortest trip, and steps that would leave it are disabled. It lists 7 among the easy flags (the worksheet's Problem 1 pair, 3 against 7: six moves, URRRDR or RURRRD), 9, 15 and 17 among the medium ones, a locked-left variant of 23 (11 moves against 10) and two "Can't" tasks (16 in 7 moves and 23 in 9, certified by the height table); none of these is built. The budget slots make "can't" implicit (a wasted move is a dead end at once), but a child never has to certify an impossibility.
- **Repeated answers.** Puzzle 8 (farthest in 9) has the same unique trip as puzzle 6 (flag at 24), and puzzle 12 (farthest in 11) is puzzle 10's trip without its last step. Restoring flag 20 (three trips, one with a step on the way down) in place of flag 24, or asking farthest in 10 (32, two trips at levels 3 and 4), would avoid the first.
- **A demo.** The plan gives each family a few-second demo of its move; a demo here would ride up two levels and take one step of 4.
- **Story.** Doubling elevators is not on the Lantern Road and has no keeper lines; Plume's count would fit, since every puzzle has a proved par.
