# Snug spinners

October 17, 2026. Nine puzzles and a playground built from Week 59 of the Bellingham math circle (constant width). They are in the puzzle satchel only, not on the Lantern Road. Nothing here has been played by children, and the Week 59 worksheet is unpiloted. The theme's [review card](https://github.com/jamesrp/math-circle-worksheets/blob/main/plans/review/week-59.md) says keep, and its Decision (October 10, 2026) is what this family builds: the child builds a shape that spins snug by choosing each arc's centre for given corners (an equilateral triangle, a regular pentagon, then irregular pentagons); the app draws it and spins it between rails; a rail that never moves certifies success and the turn where the gap changes certifies failure. Turning ready-made shapes between closing rails is the playground, with no millimetre reading and no prediction before the spin. The card's missing K–1 route stays a should. The Week 59 packet was last changed on October 6, before the card, so this is built from the current packet.

## The mathematics

**Width.** For a shape K and a unit direction u, the width of K in direction u is the gap between the two lines perpendicular to u that touch K on either side: w(u) = h(u) + h(−u), where h(u) = max of x·u over K is the support function. K has **constant width** w when w(u) = w for every u. Then K turns between two fixed parallel rails a distance w apart while touching both; if the width changes, the top rail resting on the shape moves as it turns.

**Theorem (Reuleaux polygons).** Let P₀, P₁, …, P₂ₖ be the corners of a convex polygon with an odd number n = 2k + 1 of corners, in order round it, such that every *star diagonal* has the same length w: |PᵢPᵢ₊ₖ| = w for all i (indices mod n), so each corner is w from the two corners "across" from it, Pᵢ₊ₖ and Pᵢ₊ₖ₊₁. Join each pair of neighbours Pᵢ, Pᵢ₊₁ by the arc of radius w centred at the corner across from that gap, Pᵢ₊ₖ₊₁. The resulting curved polygon has constant width w.

*Why.* The shape is the intersection of the disks of radius w about the corners. Take the arc centred at a corner C, and a direction u among that arc's outward normals. One rail touches the arc at C + w·u, as far as the disk about C reaches. The two arcs that meet at C are centred at the two corners across from C, which are that arc's ends, so their normals at C are exactly the reversed directions of the arc's normals: the opposite rail, perpendicular to u, touches the shape at C. The rails are w apart. Every direction is among some arc's normals or the reverse of them, so the width is w every way. For the triangle (n = 3, k = 1) this is the Reuleaux triangle of the worksheet's Problems 3–4; the worksheet's mathematical notes give the full support-sector proof with the whole-body checks.

**Assumptions and limits.** Odd n is essential: with an even number of corners no single corner sits across from each gap, and the construction does not exist (on a square, each corner across from a side is nearer one end of it than the other). Equal star diagonals are what let every arc join its two corners with the one radius w. The puzzles do not claim that every shape of constant width is a Reuleaux polygon (it is not: a disk, and many smooth shapes, are not), only that among the centres each puzzle offers, exactly one choice is snug. Constant width is not constant distance from a centre: on the curved triangle the centre of its corners bobs up and down as it turns (the worksheet's Problem 5), which the playground shows.

**What the child does.** Each gap between neighbouring corners needs one arc, drawn like a compass round a centre the child chooses among the corners and a few dots. A centre is allowed only when it is exactly the same distance from both ends of the gap (otherwise the compass circle misses the far corner, and the board shows by how much). Among the allowed centres:

- the **corner across** gives radius w and, with every gap done that way, the Reuleaux polygon: snug;
- the **dot halfway along the gap** gives a half circle on that side, which bulges out by half the side: the width changes (it is wider across a bulge than corner to bulge);
- a **dot inside**, on the gap's perpendicular bisector between its middle and the corner across, gives an arc of smaller radius than w, centred off the opposite rail: the width changes.

**The solve, exactly.** A shape is a solve when every gap has an arc, every arc is centred on a corner, and all the arcs have the same radius. All three are decided in exact arithmetic: corners and dots are points with coordinates a + b√r (a, b rational, r square-free) and "the same distance" compares squared distances exactly. The equilateral triangle (side 2) lies in Q(√3). The regular pentagon lies in Q(√5) once its x coordinates carry the factor cos 18°, whose square (5 + √5)/8 is in the field. The irregular pentagons are made by walking a closed five-pointed star of unit steps: e₀ = 1, two steps e₁, e₂ that are rational points of the unit circle (from Pythagorean triples 3-4-5, 8-15-17, 20-21-29, 7-24-25 and 33-56-65), and the two unit steps e₃, e₄ with e₃ + e₄ = −(e₀ + e₁ + e₂), which lie in Q(√r) for the square-free part r of (4 − |S|²)|S|², where S = e₀ + e₁ + e₂. The star's points, taken in the order 0, 3, 1, 4, 2, are the corners of a convex pentagon with all five star diagonals of length 1 (checked exactly by the builder and the validator). Floating point is used only to draw, to animate the spin and to choose which turn to stop at when showing a failure.

**Accept every valid solution.** For every puzzle the validator enumerates every full shape the child can make (4 to 32 of them) and measures its width by sampling the boundary densely in 720 directions, a different method from the mechanic's support function. Exactly one has constant width, the witness, which is also the only one the mechanic counts as a solve; every other one moves the top rail at least 8 board pixels from where it started (the least is 8.3 px, the regular pentagon with half circles on all five sides).

Experiments come first: build, spin, watch the rail. The conjecture (use the corner across; the radius must be the shape's width) comes from failures with half circles and dots inside. The explanation (a rail on an arc and a rail through its centre are one radius apart) is a grown-up conversation in each puzzle's notes; the worksheet's written proof (Problem 4) and Barbier's perimeter argument (Problem 6) stay on paper.

## How a puzzle plays

- **The board.** Corners are sun discs with their letters, dots are small ink dots, and each gap without an arc is a dashed line between its corners. The chosen gap glows pine, and on a fresh board the first empty gap is chosen. Arcs are ink; the shape so far is lightly filled.
- **Building.** Tap a gap (its dashed line or its arc) to choose it, then tap a corner or dot to centre that gap's arc there. A centre the same distance from both ends draws the arc, and the next empty gap is chosen. Any other centre draws the compass circle from the gap's first corner as an ochre dashed curve, with a short ochre bar from the circle to the corner it missed; nothing is saved and Undo has nothing new to take back. A tap on an end of the chosen gap does nothing. The chosen arc's compass shows as two faint radii from its centre. A tap goes to the nearest corner or dot within reach (they sit on top), and otherwise to the nearest gap; a dot on a gap's dashed line takes the tap at the dot. Tab reaches every gap, corner and dot, and Enter or Space taps it.
- **The spin.** As soon as every gap has an arc, the shape drops onto the bottom rail, the top rail comes down onto it, and it turns. A snug shape turns all the way round and the top rail never leaves the dashed line where it started; the last frame keeps three faint outlines of other turns between the same rails, and the touching points are pine. A shape that is not snug turns until the gap has changed the most (within half a turn) and stops there: the top rail has left the dashed line, the band between them is ochre, and ochre dots mark where the rails touch, which points at the arcs to look at. With reduced motion the last frame is shown at once.
- **Changing an arc.** Tap an arc in the spin to go back to building with that gap chosen; centring it elsewhere spins the new shape. **Spin** (above the board) plays the turn again; it is off until the shape is full. Undo takes back the last arc, and the shape before it spins again.
- **Copy.** One short objective, since the rails are not on the board while building: "Make a shape that spins snug between two rails." (builds) or "Fix the shape so it spins snug between two rails." (repairs). How to play opens with the definition of width and snug, then the taps.
- **Hints.** Before any move, the first hint is an authored nudge that names no centre ("When the top rail touches an arc, what is touching the bottom rail?"). After that a hint names one arc: a wrong arc first, otherwise the first empty gap ("Centre the arc from C to D on A."). The second level marks that gap and ringed corner on the board (on the spin too), and the third offers Apply hint. Hints alone finish every puzzle from a fresh start and after every wrong first arc.

## Certificates and claims

There is no Can't in this family: every puzzle has a snug answer, and nothing asks the child to claim "impossible", "only" or "fewest". The two certificates are the ones the card names, and the app draws both because the card's Decision makes the spin the app's job: the child acts (chooses centres), and the spin is the physical test of Problems 1–2, not a prediction.

- **Success** is a rail that never moves: the spin turns the snug shape through a whole turn with the top rail on its starting line, and the last frame keeps three other turns between the same rails. The solve itself is decided exactly from the centres, never from the picture.
- **Failure** is a turn where the gap has changed: the frame where the top rail is furthest from its starting line, with the band between them and the touching points. The validator checks that this band is at least 8 board pixels for every wrong shape.
- **A compass miss** is not a refusal of a claim but the geometry of the move: the circle round that centre does not reach the other corner. It is shown at the tap, never saved, and gives no count or value. A centre that is the same distance from both ends but wrong is not refused: it is drawn, and the spin shows why it fails.
- Brute force is bounded (at most 32 full shapes in a build), and every failure shows where the shape is wrong; whether children work that way is the first thing on the list below.

## The playground

The worksheet's five pieces (disk, oval, square, triangle, curved triangle) and two curved pentagons (the regular one and puzzle 6's lopsided one), all 60 units wide at their widest, as the worksheet's 60 mm. Choose a shape with the buttons above the board (a move, so Undo brings back the previous shape). Drag round the shape, use the turn buttons, or the arrow keys on the board to turn it; the bottom rail stays, the top rail rests on the shape, the dashed line is where it started, and a soft band shows the highest and lowest the top rail has been for this shape (the gap gauge, with no numbers). The mark O is the centre of the corners (the centre of the disk, oval and square): on the curved triangle it bobs while the rails stay still, which is Problem 5. There is no goal and no Hint, and it never counts as solved.

## Rules that keep the record honest

- The save is `{arcs}`: one entry per gap, the name of its centre or null. The view (building or spinning, the chosen gap, a miss, Spin replays, the playground's turn and band) is view state outside the save. Everything shown, including the verdict and the stopping turn, is derived from the arcs.
- Only legal moves are accepted: an arc on a gap that exists, centred on a corner or dot exactly the same distance from both its ends and not one of them, and different from the centre it has; in the playground, a different shape from the list.
- Saved boards are checked: exactly the key `arcs`, the right number of entries, each null or a centre allowed for its gap. A forged `done`, a centre that misses, an end as centre, an unknown name and a wrong length are all rejected; the playground's save is exactly `{shape}`.
- The answers, the exact checks (convexity, equal star diagonals, which points each gap allows, dots inside), the least drift, the least miss and the spacing are computed by `scripts/build-width.mjs` with the mechanic's own functions, and checked again by `scripts/validate-width.mjs` with its own exact arithmetic and a sampled boundary.

## Not yet done

- **Children's play.** Nothing here has been tried by children, and the worksheet is unpiloted. Three things to watch: whether children read the spin (the rail leaving its dashed line, the ochre band and touching points) as telling them which arc to change, or try centres at random until a spin is snug; whether the compass miss (the dashed circle and the short bar) is understood as "this centre is not the same distance from both corners", or as a refusal to puzzle over; and whether the corner across is discovered from the triangle and carried to the pentagons, or whether the mixed letters from puzzle 5 on leave children hunting.
- **Difficulty.** Once the rule "centre on the corner across" is found, the later builds are applications of it; Hard adds near-degenerate corners, mixed letters and inside dots, not a new idea. A harder tier could give corners whose star diagonals are not all equal (no snug curved polygon on those corners) with a checked certificate, or ask for the extra corners that make a given lopsided pentagon snug.
- **Rehearsal.** The spin animation, the miss drawing and the tap targets have been checked in headless Chromium at 375 × 667 and 1024 × 768, not on real phones or tablets, and not with real fingers on the short gaps of puzzles 6 and 7.
- **Story.** Not on the Lantern Road; no keeper lines.
- **Grade levels.** Grade-free; not yet in a K–1, 2–3 or 4–5 trail. The card's missing K–1 route stays a should: there is no K–1 version (for example, choosing which of two ready-made shapes keeps the rail still).
- **Paper only.** The worksheet's proof that the curved triangle has constant width (Problem 4), the marked point's distances (Problem 5's numbers) and Barbier's perimeter comparison (Problem 6) stay on paper; the playground only shows O bobbing.
