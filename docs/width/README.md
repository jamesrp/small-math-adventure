# Snug spinners

October 17, 2026. Nine puzzles and a playground built from Week 59 of the Bellingham math circle (constant width). They are in the puzzle satchel only, not on the Lantern Road. Nothing here has been played by children, and the Week 59 worksheet is unpiloted. The theme's [review card](https://github.com/jamesrp/math-circle-worksheets/blob/main/plans/review/week-59.md) says keep, and its Decision (October 10, 2026) is what this family builds: the child builds a shape that spins snug by choosing each arc's centre for given corners (an equilateral triangle, a regular pentagon, then irregular pentagons); the app draws it and spins it between rails; a rail that never moves certifies success and the turn where the gap changes certifies failure. Turning ready-made shapes between closing rails is the playground, with no millimetre reading and no prediction before the spin. The card's missing K–1 route stays a should. The Week 59 packet was last changed on October 6, before the card, so this is built from the current packet. After review (October 17) the Hard tier was replaced: instead of more builds of the same kind, the child first puts a missing corner on one of six spots and then builds the arcs, so Hard asks a new question (where must a corner be?) rather than repeating Easy and Medium.

## The mathematics

**Width.** For a shape K and a unit direction u, the width of K in direction u is the gap between the two lines perpendicular to u that touch K on either side: w(u) = h(u) + h(−u), where h(u) = max of x·u over K is the support function. K has **constant width** w when w(u) = w for every u. Then K turns between two fixed parallel rails a distance w apart while touching both; if the width changes, the top rail resting on the shape moves as it turns.

**Theorem (Reuleaux polygons).** Let P₀, P₁, …, P₂ₖ be the corners of a convex polygon with an odd number n = 2k + 1 of corners, in order round it, such that every *star diagonal* has the same length w: |PᵢPᵢ₊ₖ| = w for all i (indices mod n), so each corner is w from the two corners "across" from it, Pᵢ₊ₖ and Pᵢ₊ₖ₊₁. Join each pair of neighbours Pᵢ, Pᵢ₊₁ by the arc of radius w centred at the corner across from that gap, Pᵢ₊ₖ₊₁. The resulting curved polygon has constant width w.

*Why.* The shape is the intersection of the disks of radius w about the corners. Take the arc centred at a corner C, and a direction u among that arc's outward normals. One rail touches the arc at C + w·u, as far as the disk about C reaches. The two arcs that meet at C are centred at the two corners across from C, which are that arc's ends, so their normals at C are exactly the reversed directions of the arc's normals: the opposite rail, perpendicular to u, touches the shape at C. The rails are w apart. Every direction is among some arc's normals or the reverse of them, so the width is w every way. For the triangle (n = 3, k = 1) this is the Reuleaux triangle of the worksheet's Problems 3–4; the worksheet's mathematical notes give the full support-sector proof with the whole-body checks.

**Where a missing corner goes.** Take a Reuleaux pentagon P₀ … P₄ of width w and hide one corner Pₒ. Its arcs are the arc from Pₒ₋₁ to Pₒ, centred on Pₒ₊₂, the arc from Pₒ to Pₒ₊₁, centred on Pₒ₊₃, and the arc from Pₒ₊₂ to Pₒ₊₃, centred on Pₒ. For each to have radius w, Pₒ must be exactly w from Pₒ₊₂ and from Pₒ₊₃: on the circle of radius w round Pₒ₊₂ (which passes through Pₒ₋₁) and on the one round Pₒ₊₃ (which passes through Pₒ₊₁). Two circles of equal radius meet in at most two points, mirror images across the line through their centres; Pₒ₊₂Pₒ₊₃ is a side of the pentagon, and the mirror point lies beyond that side, where no corner of a convex pentagon in this order can be. So the four corners fix the fifth. The puzzles offer five other spots, and for each of them the builder and validator check that at least one of those three arcs has no centre among the corners that is the same distance from both its ends: its compass misses.

**Assumptions and limits.** Odd n is essential: with an even number of corners no single corner sits across from each gap, and the construction does not exist (on a square, each corner across from a side is nearer one end of it than the other). Equal star diagonals are what let every arc join its two corners with the one radius w. The puzzles do not claim that every shape of constant width is a Reuleaux polygon (it is not: a disk, and many smooth shapes, are not), only that among the centres each puzzle offers, exactly one choice is snug. Constant width is not constant distance from a centre: on the curved triangle the centre of its corners bobs up and down as it turns (the worksheet's Problem 5), which the playground shows.

**What the child does.** Each gap between neighbouring corners needs one arc, drawn like a compass round a centre the child chooses among the corners and a few dots. A centre is allowed only when it is exactly the same distance from both ends of the gap (otherwise the compass circle misses the far corner, and the board shows by how much). Among the allowed centres:

- the **corner across** gives radius w and, with every gap done that way, the Reuleaux polygon: snug;
- the **dot halfway along the gap** gives a half circle on that side, which bulges out by half the side: the width changes (on the triangle of side 2 with all three half circles, the shape is 3 wide along a side and 2 + √3/2 ≈ 2.87 across it, and no corner ever touches a rail; a unit test checks these numbers);
- a **dot inside**, on the gap's perpendicular bisector between its middle and the corner across, gives an arc of smaller radius than w, centred off the opposite rail: the width changes.

In the hard puzzles four corners and two of the arcs are given (the two that neither end at the missing corner nor are centred on it), and six hollow spots lie near where the fifth corner goes: the corner itself, two spots on the circle round one of the corners across from it only, two on the circle round the other only, and one on the perpendicular bisector of those two corners, nearer than the width. The child taps a spot to put the corner there and then builds the three missing arcs. On a wrong spot the corner's compass misses on at least one of them (the validator checks that no full shape can be made there at all, so there is never a spin to read and never a yes or no without the child trying an arc). A missed compass is drawn from the corner that stays put and runs on past every spot, so it shows which spots lie on that circle.

**The solve, exactly.** A shape is a solve when every corner is in place, every gap has an arc, every arc is centred on a corner, and all the arcs have the same radius. All of these are decided in exact arithmetic: corners, dots and spots are points with coordinates a + b√r (a, b rational, r square-free) and "the same distance" compares squared distances exactly. The equilateral triangle (side 2) lies in Q(√3). The regular pentagon lies in Q(√5) once its x coordinates carry the factor cos 18°, whose square (5 + √5)/8 is in the field. The irregular pentagons are made by walking a closed five-pointed star of unit steps: e₀ = 1, two steps e₁, e₂ that are rational points of the unit circle (from Pythagorean triples 3-4-5, 5-12-13, 8-15-17, 11-60-61, 28-45-53 and 48-55-73), and the two unit steps e₃, e₄ with e₃ + e₄ = −(e₀ + e₁ + e₂), which lie in Q(√r) for the square-free part r of (4 − |S|²)|S|², where S = e₀ + e₁ + e₂. The star's points, taken in the order 0, 3, 1, 4, 2, are the corners of a convex pentagon with all five star diagonals of length 1 (checked exactly by the builder and the validator). The hard puzzles' wrong spots are exact too: the corner turned round one of the corners across by a rational rotation (cos, sin from the triples 9-40-41, 7-24-25 and 11-60-61; twice for the second spot on the same circle), and the point 4/5 of the way from the middle of the two corners across to the corner. Floating point is used only to draw, to animate the spin and to choose which turn to stop at when showing a failure.

**Accept every valid solution.** For every puzzle the validator enumerates every full shape the child can make (4 to 32 of them in the builds and repairs; in the hard puzzles, for the corner on each of the six spots) and measures its width by sampling the boundary densely in 720 directions, a different method from the mechanic's support function. Exactly one has constant width, the witness, which is also the only one the mechanic counts as a solve; every other one moves the top rail at least 8 board pixels from where it started (the least is 8.3 px, the regular pentagon with half circles on all five sides). In the hard puzzles exactly one spot is the width from both corners across (checked exactly), no full shape at all can be made on the other five, and a blind sweep (every spot tried, and on each wrong one the corner's arcs until a compass misses) costs at most 6 placements, no spins and 19 taps. Reading the circles that missed compasses draw can find the spot without a sweep; whether children do that is untested.

Experiments come first: build, spin, watch the rail. The conjecture (use the corner across; the radius must be the shape's width) comes from failures with half circles and dots inside, and the hard puzzles turn it round: if every arc must have that radius, where must a corner be? The explanation (a rail on an arc and a rail through its centre are one radius apart) is a grown-up conversation in each puzzle's notes; the worksheet's written proof (Problem 4) and Barbier's perimeter argument (Problem 6) stay on paper.

## Where they appear

**Puzzles → Snug spinners** (symbol ◬) is a satchel family on the family seam ([Adding a family](../ADDING-A-FAMILY.md)), with a **Playground** button above Easy, Medium and Hard. The puzzles are grade-free (`band: "all"`). Corners are lettered in order round the shape (counterclockwise) in puzzles 1 to 4; from puzzle 5 on the letters are mixed, so the answer is found by looking, not from a pattern of letters. The levels follow the number of full shapes a child can make: Easy has 8, 4 and 4, Medium 32, 32 and 16, and Hard asks a new question. "m" dots are halfway along a gap; "i" dots are inside, a fraction t of the way from a gap's middle to the corner across.

## How a puzzle plays

- **The board.** Corners are sun discs with their letters, dots are small ink dots, spots (hard puzzles) are hollow rings, and each gap without an arc is a dashed line between its corners. While a corner is missing, a dotted line joins its two neighbours. The chosen gap glows pine, and on a fresh board the first empty gap is chosen. Arcs are ink; the shape so far is lightly filled.
- **Placing a corner (hard).** Tap a spot to put the missing corner there; it becomes a lettered disc and its two gaps appear. Tap another spot to move it: the arcs that end at it or are centred on it go with it (Undo brings them back).
- **Building.** Tap a gap (its dashed line or its arc) to choose it, then tap a corner or dot to centre that gap's arc there. A centre the same distance from both ends draws the arc, and the next empty gap is chosen. Any other centre draws the compass circle as an ochre dashed curve from the gap's corner that stays put (the first, or the other one when the first is the corner being placed), with a short ochre bar from the circle to the corner it missed; while a corner is being placed the circle runs on past every spot. Nothing is saved and Undo has nothing new to take back. A tap on an end of the chosen gap does nothing. The chosen arc's compass shows as two faint radii from its centre.
- **Taps.** A tap inside a corner's disc (radius 12.5, tap radius 14), a dot (5.5, tap radius 9) or a spot (8, tap radius 12) goes to it. A little further out (up to 17) it goes to the point only when it is nearer the point's drawn edge than any gap's line; otherwise it goes to the nearest gap or arc within 22. So a tap on a short gap's line just outside the discs at its ends chooses the gap, and a dot on a gap's dashed line takes only the taps on the dot. Tab reaches every gap, corner, dot and spot, and Enter or Space taps it.
- **The spin.** As soon as every gap has an arc, the shape drops onto the bottom rail, the top rail comes down onto it, and it turns. A snug shape turns all the way round and the top rail never leaves the dashed line where it started; the last frame keeps three faint outlines of other turns between the same rails, and the touching points are pine. A shape that is not snug turns until the gap has changed the most (within half a turn) and stops there: the top rail has left the dashed line, the band between them is ochre, and ochre dots mark where the rails touch, which points at the arcs to look at. A tap or key during the spin ends it on that last frame and does nothing else. With reduced motion nothing moves: the shape rests unturned between the rails for a moment, then the last frame appears, so Spin always shows a change.
- **Changing an arc.** Tap an arc in the spin to go back to building with that gap chosen; centring it elsewhere spins the new shape. **Spin** (above the board) plays the turn again; it is off until the shape is full. Undo takes back the last step, and the shape before it spins again.
- **Copy.** One short objective, since the rails are not on the board while building: "Make a shape that spins snug between two rails." (builds), "Fix the shape so it spins snug between two rails." (repairs) or "Put corner E on a spot, then make the shape spin snug." (hard). How to play opens with the definition of width and snug, then the taps.
- **Hints.** Before any move, the first hint is an authored nudge that names no centre ("When the top rail touches an arc, what is touching the bottom rail?"; in the hard puzzles "The two arcs already drawn have the radius every arc needs. Where could corner E go so that its arcs have that radius too?"). After that a hint asks a question about one step and never names its answer: a wrong arc first ("The arc from C to D is centred on a dot. Which corner could the compass reach both C and D from?"), otherwise the first empty gap ("Which corner could the compass reach both A and B from?"), and in the hard puzzles, while the corner is not on its spot, "Which spot is as far from B as A is, and as far from D as C is?". The second level marks that gap and rings the centre (or the spot) on the board, on the spin too, and the third offers Apply hint. Hints alone finish every puzzle from a fresh start, after every wrong first arc and after every wrong spot.

## Certificates and claims

There is no Can't in this family: every puzzle has a snug answer, and nothing asks the child to claim "impossible", "only" or "fewest". The two certificates are the ones the card names, and the app draws both because the card's Decision makes the spin the app's job: the child acts (chooses centres), and the spin is the physical test of Problems 1–2, not a prediction.

- **Success** is a rail that never moves: the spin turns the snug shape through a whole turn with the top rail on its starting line, and the last frame keeps three other turns between the same rails. The solve itself is decided exactly from the centres, never from the picture.
- **Failure** is a turn where the gap has changed: the frame where the top rail is furthest from its starting line, with the band between them and the touching points. The validator checks that this band is at least 8 board pixels for every wrong shape.
- **A compass miss** is not a refusal of a claim but the geometry of the move: the circle round that centre does not reach the other corner. It is shown at the tap, never saved, and gives no count or value. A centre that is the same distance from both ends but wrong is not refused: it is drawn, and the spin shows why it fails.
- **A wrong spot** is shown the same way: on it, at least one of the missing corner's three arcs has no corner the same distance from both its ends, so its compass misses, and no full shape (so no spin) can be made there. The child sees that by trying an arc, never from a verdict on the spot itself.
- Brute force is bounded (at most 32 full shapes in a build; in a hard puzzle 6 spots, no spins and at most 19 taps), and every failure shows where the shape is wrong; whether children work that way is the first thing on the list below.

## The playground

The worksheet's five pieces (disk, oval, square, triangle, curved triangle) and two curved pentagons (the regular one and puzzle 6's lopsided one), all 60 units wide at their widest, as the worksheet's 60 mm. Choose a shape with the buttons above the board (a move, so Undo brings back the previous shape); each button shows the shape with a dot at each corner, so the curved triangle (three) and the lopsided curved pentagon (five) look different. Drag round the shape, use the turn buttons, or the arrow keys on the board to turn it; the bottom rail stays, the top rail rests on the shape, the dashed line is where it started, and a soft band shows the highest and lowest the top rail has been for this shape (the gap gauge, with no numbers). The mark O is the centre of the corners (the centre of the disk, oval and square): on the curved triangle it bobs while the rails stay still, which is Problem 5. There is no goal and no Hint, and it never counts as solved.

## Rules that keep the record honest

- The save is `{arcs}`, or `{corner, arcs}` in the hard puzzles (the spot the missing corner is on, or null): one entry per gap, the name of its centre or null. The view (building or spinning, the chosen gap, a miss, Spin replays, the playground's turn and band) is view state outside the save. Everything shown, including the verdict and the stopping turn, is derived from the save.
- Only legal moves are accepted: an arc on a gap that exists (with both its corners there), centred on a corner or dot exactly the same distance from both its ends and not one of them, and different from the centre it has; the missing corner onto a spot it is not on (never a corner name, and never in a puzzle with every corner); in the playground, a different shape from the list.
- Saved boards are checked: exactly the key `arcs` (or `corner` and `arcs`), the right number of entries, each null or a centre allowed for its gap with the corner where the save puts it. A forged `done`, a centre that misses, an end as centre, an unknown name, a wrong length, a spot as a centre, arcs to a corner not placed and the right arcs on a wrong spot are all rejected; the playground's save is exactly `{shape}`.
- The answers, the exact checks (convexity, equal star diagonals, which points each gap allows, dots inside, every spot keeping the corners convex), the least drift, the least miss and the spacing are computed by `scripts/build-width.mjs` with the mechanic's own functions, and checked again by `scripts/validate-width.mjs` with its own exact arithmetic and a sampled boundary, spot by spot in the hard puzzles.

## Not yet done

- **Children's play.** Nothing here has been tried by children, and the worksheet is unpiloted. Things to watch: whether children read the spin (the rail leaving its dashed line, the ochre band and touching points) as telling them which arc to change, or try centres at random until a spin is snug; whether the compass miss (the dashed circle and the short bar) is understood as "this centre is not the same distance from both corners", or as a refusal to puzzle over; whether the corner across is discovered from the triangle and carried to the pentagons, or whether the mixed letters from puzzle 5 on leave children hunting; and, in the hard puzzles, whether children use the circle a missed compass draws to see which spots lie on it, or sweep the six spots one by one (at most 19 taps).
- **Difficulty.** Easy and Medium apply one rule, "centre on the corner across"; Hard asks where a corner must be for that rule to give one radius. The hard puzzles' difficulty is untested, and a sweep of six spots is cheap; more spots, or spots on both circles near the crossing, would make the sweep dearer. A harder tier still could give corners whose star diagonals are not all equal (no snug curved polygon on them) with a checked certificate. Whether the levels (Easy 8, 4, 4 full shapes; Medium 32, 32, 16) match what children find hard is untested.
- **Rehearsal.** The spin animation, the reduced-motion Spin, the miss drawing and the tap targets have been checked in headless Chromium at 375 × 667 and 1024 × 768 (including taps along every short gap just outside its end discs), not on real phones or tablets and not with real fingers.
- **Story.** Not on the Lantern Road; no keeper lines.
- **Grade levels.** Grade-free; not yet in a K–1, 2–3 or 4–5 trail. The card's missing K–1 route stays a should: there is no K–1 version (for example, choosing which of two ready-made shapes keeps the rail still).
- **Paper only.** The worksheet's proof that the curved triangle has constant width (Problem 4), the marked point's distances (Problem 5's numbers) and Barbier's perimeter comparison (Problem 6) stay on paper; the playground only shows O bobbing.
