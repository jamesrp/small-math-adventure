# Trios

October 10, 2026. Twelve puzzles and a playground built from Week 36 of the Bellingham math circle (Making threes) and its bonus page. They are in the puzzle satchel only, not on the Lantern Road. Nothing here has been played by children, and the Week 36 worksheet is unpiloted. The theme's [review card](https://github.com/jamesrp/math-circle-worksheets/blob/main/plans/review/week-36.md) says port at size M, and its Decision shaped this family: the nine tiles as the tutorial and the find-every-split set, then the 27-card layer where "reach 9" is a real solve and each unchosen card lights with the pair that blocks it, plus the bonus avoidance game and the three-colour puzzle, with tile positions shuffled so board lines don't stand in for the rule.

## The mathematics

A **card** has two, three or four attributes, each with three values: shape (circle, triangle, square) and fill (open, striped, solid) for the nine **tiles**; then a count of one, two or three shapes for 27 cards; then an ink colour (green, red, blue) for 81. A **trio** is three different cards whose values, attribute by attribute, are all the same or all different. Writing each value as 0, 1 or 2, three values are all the same or all different exactly when their sum is divisible by 3. So the cards are the points of the affine space over the integers mod 3 (dimension 2, 3 or 4), and the trios are its lines.

- **Two cards have exactly one third card.** In each attribute, keep the value the two share, or take the value neither has: the third card is −(x + y) mod 3 coordinate by coordinate, and it is always in the deck. So two trios share at most one card (puzzles 1 and 6).
- **The nine tiles: 12 trios, 4 through each tile, 4 splits.** The eight other tiles pair off, each pair making a trio with a given tile, so four trios pass through it (puzzle 2). Two trios with no tile in common change their attributes the same way, so the trios fall into four families of three disjoint trios; these are the only ways to split the nine tiles into three trios (puzzle 4). They are the plane's four parallel classes: by shape, by fill, and two mixed splits that read as a pair of orthogonal Latin squares of order 3.
- **At most four tiles hold no trio, and every stuck set has four.** 54 of the 126 fours hold no trio (puzzle 3). Five tiles always hold one or two: their ten pairs each have a third tile; for a five with no trio it would be one of the four others, and each of those completes at most two of the pairs (two pairs it completes can't share a tile), so 8 < 10 (puzzle 5, the guide's "ten pair markers in four dishes"). Any three tiles with no trio leave exactly three tiles that can join, so choosing one tile at a time always stops at four, never sooner. So puzzle 3 is the largest-set question in all but name: every set of tiles with no trio that can't be added to has four tiles, and 54 of the 126 fours solve it. The word "largest" is left out and the four dots give the size; that four is the most comes from failing at five (puzzle 5), not from a statement.
- **Two colours fail; three work.** Each colour's tiles may hold no trio, so each colour has at most four tiles, and 4 + 4 < 9. Every two-colouring has at least two one-colour trios (puzzle 7). With three colours, 3,762 colourings work, 1,008 of them with three tiles of each colour (puzzle 8).
- **Counts with no trio.** Giving each tile a count of one, two or three shapes turns it into one of the 27 cards. In 162 of the 19,683 ways no three tiles make a trio as cards (no trio of tiles all one count or with three different counts), and every one uses one count once and the other two four times (puzzle 11). One is count − 1 = x² + y² mod 3 with shapes and fills numbered 0, 1, 2, the bonus guide's design. Each such design is a nine-card set with no trio among the 27.
- **The 27 cards: nine is the most, but stuck sets have eight or nine.** There are 117 trios, 13 through each card. 2,106 nines hold no trio and no ten does (puzzle 9). A set with no trio to which no card can be added has 8 cards (44,226 sets) or 9 (2,106). Choosing at random one card at a time sticks at eight about two times in three (the review card: 13,752 of 20,000 runs; the validator: 2,714 of 4,000). Puzzle 10 starts from the stuck eight of one and two shapes, open and striped, circles and triangles: every three-shape card is the third card of a one-shape and a two-shape card in it, and no nine with no trio contains more than six of its cards, so at least two must go.
- **The 81 cards** of the card game SET have 1,080 trios, and the most cards with no trio is 20 (Pellegrino, 1970); the review card found random choices reaching 20 in 9 of 5,000 runs. This is the playground only.
- **The avoidance game: the first player wins.** Two players take turns claiming tiles; whoever first owns a trio among their own tiles loses. The first player claims any tile c, then answers each tile x the other claims with its **opposite**, the third tile of c and x. The other eight tiles fall into four opposite pairs, so the opposite is always free; the first player never holds both tiles of a pair, so never owns a trio through c, and a trio of theirs away from c would mirror one the other player already owned. The other player loses by their third or fourth tile (all 3,024 lines of play checked, from every first tile). At the first player's second turn the opposite is the only winning claim; later there are sometimes two or three. The strategy and its check are the bonus guide's (p. 2); the opposite is reflection through c. The Week 36 guide cites Tanya Khovanova's SET tic-tac-toe as a related game precedent, not as the same game.

Sets with no line are **cap sets** (Carney, *SET and Finite Affine Geometry*, section 5, as the Week 36 guide cites for the four-point maximum). The largest have 4, 9 and 20 points in dimensions 2, 3 and 4 (the first two are checked here; 20 is Pellegrino's), and Ellenberg and Gijswijt (2017) proved they grow much more slowly than 3ⁿ.

Experiments come first: tap a third tile and read the rule rows when it is wrong; choose sets and colour tiles, hunting for the trio when a full try holds one; change counts with the trios drawn as they appear; play the game and lose. Conjectures follow from finding every trio and every split, from failing at five, and from sticking at eight. The explanations (pairing off, the dishes, 4 + 4 < 9, the opposite) are grown-up conversations in each puzzle's notes; after a right Can't the app draws the dishes or the colour rows for the child's own attempt.

## How a puzzle plays

- **The board.** The nine tiles sit on a ring, so no rows or columns suggest trios, in a shuffled order where no three ring neighbours make a trio. The 27 cards sit on a grid of six columns and the 81 on nine, shuffled so that no three in a row or a column make a trio. A card shows one, two or three shapes, open, striped or solid; the 81 add ink colour. On the ring, a trio is drawn as a triangle joining its tiles under them, and tiles in a trio that breaks the goal get a brick border; on the grids the dims, rings and rule rows show trios instead of lines. Where Can't is offered (3, 5, 7, 8), a trio in the child's try is not drawn until a claim is right: see [Can't and its certificate](#cant-and-its-certificate).
- **Third tile and third card** (1, 6). Two cards are ringed. A tap on the right card puts the trio on a shelf and rings the next pair. A wrong tap marks that card and shows the three with a row per attribute: its three values and ✓ (all the same or all different) or ✗ (two the same).
- **Through one tile** (2). Tap two tiles; if they make a trio with the ringed tile it is kept and drawn through it; if not, both stay marked with the rule rows until the next tap.
- **Splits** (4). A tap puts a tile in the next tray with room; a tap on a tray tile takes it out. A full tray shows its rule rows. When the ninth tile makes three trios the split is kept on a shelf and the trays empty.
- **Find every** (2, 4). A trio or split found again gives "Found already.". **That's all** says "There is another." until every one is found, and then waits for a new find; Undo keeps what was found and that answer. There are no empty slots and no count.
- **Sets with no trio** (3, 5, 9, 10). Tap a card to choose it and again to put it back; dots show how many to choose. On the nine, a full try that holds a trio gets "There is a trio.", without showing where. Among the 27, a card that would make a trio with two chosen cards is dimmed and can't be chosen; tapping it lights the two chosen cards that rule it out, with the rule rows. When every other card is dimmed, the set is visibly stuck.
- **Colours and counts** (7, 8, 11). A tap changes a tile's colour in turn (yellow, blue, and red when there are three; the tiles start blank) or its count (one, two, three, one). With colours, a full colouring with a trio all one colour gets "A trio is all one colour.", without showing where; with counts every trio that breaks the rule is drawn.
- **The game** (12). Tap a free tile to claim it; the app answers at once with its claim. Your tiles are yellow, theirs blue, their latest with a darker border. The first to own a trio loses; the trio is drawn. Undo takes back your last turn and the app's answer. The app plays its best: it takes a winning claim when it has one and avoids owning a trio while it can. At your second turn the opposite is the only claim that keeps the win.
- **Copy.** Each puzzle shows one short objective, since the board can't show the rule: "Tap the tile that makes a trio.", "Find every trio with the ringed tile.", "Choose four tiles with no trio.", "Find every way to split the tiles into three trios.", "Choose five tiles with no trio.", "Tap the card that makes a trio.", "Colour every tile so no trio is all one colour.", "Choose nine cards with no trio.", "Change counts until no trio is left." and "Take turns with the app; the first to own a trio loses." How to play gives the trio rule and the controls.
- **Hints.** Before any move, the first hint is the authored nudge, which never names Can't or an answer. Then each hint is one move toward the nearest finish, in words: third cards name the rule attribute by attribute ("Circle and circle: circle; open and striped: solid. Tap the solid circle."); sets aim at the goal set sharing the most chosen cards, taking out a card first; colourings aim at the nearest working colouring; splits take a wrong tile out of a tray first. The second level marks the card, and the third offers Apply hint. In the game, from a position the app can always win, the hint (only when asked) is "From here the app can always win. Undo your last turn." and Apply hint takes the turn back. Hints alone finish every puzzle, from a fresh start, after wrong taps, after a wrong That's all, after a wrong Can't and after random play.

## Can't and its certificate

Puzzles 3, 5, 7 and 8 have **Can't**. It is in all four so that its presence gives nothing away: four tiles and three colours can be done, five tiles and two colours can't.

- **The trio is the child's to find.** In these four puzzles a full try that fails is reported ("There is a trio.", "A trio is all one colour.") but its trio is not drawn, ringed or named, on screen or to a screen reader, until a claim is right. Drawing it would let a child finish the certificate by tapping whatever is marked; in puzzle 11 and among the 27, which have no Can't, trios are drawn or lit as before.
- **Can't comes after an attempt.** It is enabled only once the try is full (as many tiles chosen as the dots show, or every tile coloured). Press Can't ("Tap the trio." appears), then tap three of your tiles that make a trio (puzzles 3 and 5) or three tiles that make a trio all one colour (7 and 8). The child supplies the certificate: a trio in their own full try. The certificate can fail: while Can't is on, every tile can be tapped, nothing but the child's own taps is marked, and the claim is judged when the third tile is in.
- **A wrong certificate** gets one short refusal, which clears the taps and turns Can't off: "Not a trio." for three tiles that are not a trio, "Not a trio in your four." (or five) for a real trio not all among the chosen tiles, and "Not a trio all one colour." with colours. Every wrong claim is judged and refused; none is ignored.
- **A right certificate where the goal can be done** gets "There is a way.", said once, right after the claim. The refusal is recorded in the save: Can't stays off for the rest of the attempt, through Undo, later moves and Restart, so Can't-then-Undo can't be used to ask whether a goal is possible. One Undo right after the claim takes back the tile before it too. After the next move, Undo or Restart the usual note returns, so a later full try with a trio again says "There is a trio." or "A trio is all one colour.".
- **Undo leaves Can't mode.** Undo turns Can't off and clears its taps (and any lit pair).
- **A right certificate where the goal can't be done** solves the puzzle, and the app then draws the full argument round the child's own try. For five tiles: the four other tiles as dishes, each holding the pairs of the five whose third tile it is (never more than two), and the pairs left over, which lie inside trios. For two colours: each colour's tiles in a row with a frame of room for four, one colour spilling past it. These are drawn rather than tapped because the argument is a count over all fives or all colourings, which no single tap can point at; the tappable part, the trio in the child's try, is what the child supplies.
- **Hints for an impossible goal** first build a full try (four tiles with no trio and then a fifth, or a five-and-four colouring with two one-colour trios), then give the reason with the claim: "The open circle, striped triangle and solid square make a trio." The second level marks Can't and the three tiles, and the third offers Apply hint. No hint text says "Press Can't".

The 27-card puzzles have no Can't, since nine can always be reached; their "stuck" certificate is the dimming: every unchosen card dimmed, and a tap on any of them lights the pair that blocks it.

## The playground

Choose 9, 27 or 81 cards. Tap cards to choose them; a card that would make a trio with two chosen cards is dimmed and can't be chosen, and tapping it lights the two. The number chosen is shown. Clear puts every card back. There is no goal and no Hint, and it never counts as solved. Four is the most among the 9, nine among the 27 and twenty among the 81.

## Rules that keep the record honest

- The save is the round and a wrong tap (third cards); the picked tiles, trios found, the answer to That's all and the done flag (through one tile); the trays, splits found, answer and done flag (splits); the chosen cards, a kept claim and an answer (sets); the colours or counts, a kept claim and an answer (colourings); the list of claims, the app's included (the game); or the deck and the chosen cards (playground). Everything shown is derived from it.
- Only legal moves are accepted: no tap on a ringed card; no choosing a dimmed card or more cards than the dots; no Can't before a full try, after "There is a way.", or in a puzzle without it; That's all not twice without a new find; nothing after a solve; in the game, only free tiles, and the app's answer is the app's own.
- Saved boards are checked: every card is in the deck; lists are sorted with no repeats; trios and splits found are real, found once, and never left on the board (they are kept the moment they are made); "There is another." only while one is missing; done only with every one found; a kept claim only for an impossible goal, with a full try holding that trio; "There is a way." only where the goal can be done; a refused certificate only on a full try, and "in your four" only with sets; "Found already." only before That's all is done; with dimming, no chosen trio; the game's claims replay with the app's own answers.
- The answers and witnesses are computed by `scripts/build-trios.mjs` with the mechanic's functions and checked again by `scripts/validate-trios.mjs` by other methods.

## Not yet done

- **Children's play.** Nothing here has been tried by children, and the worksheet is unpiloted. Three things to watch: whether children judge shape and fill separately or look for a tile that "goes with" a pair, and whether they read the ✓ and ✗ rows after a wrong tap; whether the Can't sequence (a full try, Can't, then tapping a trio) is found without help, whether hunting for the unshown trio after "There is a trio." is too hard for an Easy puzzle (3), and whether "There is a way." on puzzles 3 and 8 reads as information rather than a scolding, and the dishes and colour rows mean anything without an adult; and whether Nine cards and Stuck at eight feel like a search worth doing or a stall, and whether children tap dimmed cards to see the pair that rules them out.
- **Legibility.** On a 375-pixel phone the 27 cards are about 50 pixels wide with shapes of about 12 pixels, and the 81 about 32 pixels wide; counts on the ring (puzzle 11) are small too. The 81 use green, red and blue ink, which some colour-blind children can't tell apart; ink appears only in the playground.
- **The game.** The app always plays its best, so a child who doesn't find the opposite answer loses every game; there is no gentler opponent and no choice of who starts. The base packet's game (one shared set, whoever completes a trio loses; Grades 2–3 and 4–5 Problem 5) always ends at the fifth tile, so it is predict-an-outcome, as the card says, and is only a grown-up extension here.
- **Story.** Not on the Lantern Road; no keeper lines.
- **Grade levels.** Grade-free; not yet in a K–1, 2–3 or 4–5 trail. The K–1 packet's sorting and its tile-matching games have no puzzle here.
- **Paper only.** Reading the mixed splits as orthogonal Latin squares, the x² + y² design, and the written proofs stay in the worksheet and the grown-up notes. The card's cross-references (Week 19's antichains, Week 62's colourings, Symbol Orchard's Latin squares) are not linked in the app.
