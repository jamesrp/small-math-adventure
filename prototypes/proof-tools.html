Proof tools

Prototype for the Lantern Caravan: four ways a child playing alone can show an insight, each checked by the app without AI. Grown-up notes under each board say what is and is not verified.

Grown-up notes

Week 1, grades 4–5, Problem 4: “Can you leave A and return to A in exactly three flips? Five flips? Any odd number of flips? Explain what you notice.” The six cards are the worksheet’s tilings of the 2–2–1 hexagon, in the same orientation and with the same letters. Each line joins two cards one flip apart.

  • 1. Walk. A to A in exactly 4 flips. Teaches the move.
  • 2. Prove. A to A in exactly 5 flips cannot be walked. Can’t be done opens a paint tool with no instructions. Check proof accepts only a painting in which every line joins two colors and A’s color rules out an odd count. Failed checks name the broken condition, so the rule is learned from feedback. A valid painting proves the claim for every odd number at once.
  • 3. Any odd number? Tap every number of flips from 1 to 12 that can bring you from A back to A. This is the worksheet’s question as a classification, answered once.
  • 4. Apply it. Six route cards, each “from X to Y in exactly k,” sorted ✓ or ✗ in one try. Some are ruled out by the colors, some by distance (A to F in 2 has the right parity but is too short), and the rest are possible. This checks the step the painting alone does not: using the colors to decide. New cards are dealt until a round is all right.
  • Not verified: whether the child can say why a two-coloring rules out odd returns. After a proof, the dot strip shows that step: the color alternates with each flip and finishes on the wrong one.
  • Mathematical aside: in ribbon codes a flip swaps a neighboring L and R, and the number of cubes is the number of L-before-R pairs. So this parity is the parity of a permutation, the same invariant as Cup Swaps with neighboring cups.

Cover the garden with dominoes, or prove it can’t be done.

Grown-up notes

Every garden ends with a checkable object: a covering when one exists, otherwise one of two refutations.

  • Paint: touching squares get different colors and the two counts differ. Each domino covers one square of each color, so no covering exists.
  • Stars: starred squares may not touch. On Check proof, dashed rings mark every square next to a star. More stars than rings means some star has no partner left (Hall’s condition). Rings appear only on a check, so the child has to think about neighbors rather than watch a counter.
  • For dominoes on the square grid this system is complete. By Hall’s theorem every uncoverable garden has a star set that works; the coloring argument is the special case of starring one whole color. So uncoverable gardens do have a witness.
  • Garden 1 is the mutilated board. Garden 2 removes two corners of opposite color and can be covered (Gomory’s theorem), which catches “missing corners means impossible.” Garden 3 has equal colors, so paint cannot refute it; two squares share their only partner. Garden 4 has two interior holes of opposite color and can be covered.
  • Open question for playtesting: whether the star tool is usable without a demonstration. It needs a tutorial garden of its own before garden 3.

Which gardens can be covered?

Grown-up notes
  • Six fresh boards per round, from a named family and filtered for purpose. ✓ means coverable, ✗ means not. Thumbnails are uncolored on purpose; colored ones would turn the round into color matching.
  • One check per round. A miss reveals every board: a covering for each coverable one, a refutation for the rest, and the wrong answers outlined. A level is passed after two clean rounds in a row.
  • Each board’s answer is a coin flip, resampled only to guarantee the boards each round needs. A blind guesser passes one round about 1 time in 60 and a level about 1 time in 4,000. Allowing a second try with “N are wrong” feedback would raise the one-round figure to 7 in 64.
  • Each level is built against named wrong rules. Level 1 always includes two same-color holes (refutes “even number of holes ⇒ ✓”). Level 2 always includes two neighboring corners of opposite color, which can be covered (refutes “missing corners ⇒ ✗”); with two holes the color rule is exactly right (Gomory). Level 3 removes four and always includes both a stranded corner square and a color-unbalanced board with nothing stranded, so neither “colors balance ⇒ ✓” nor “stranded ⇒ ✗” survives. On 6×6 boards with four holes and balanced colors, a stranded corner is the only way to fail (540 of 23,409 boards), so random sampling alone would almost never show it.

Take the last pebble. Choose who starts.

Grown-up notes
  • Tap a pebble to take it and every pebble above it. The opponent plays perfectly. When losing, it picks the move that leaves the child the fewest winning replies.
  • Choosing who starts is the classification test, inside play: it is right only when the child can tell a losing position (Nim-sum 0) from a winning one.
  • Starts are drawn half from Nim-sum-0 positions. With three piles of 1–7, only 42 of 343 positions (12%) are of that kind, so under uniform sampling “Me first” would be right 88% of the time.
  • The streak counts only wins from a new start. Same start again is offered after a loss, for practice, and does not count. Three wins in a row from fresh starts is strong evidence of the strategy or an equivalent one.
